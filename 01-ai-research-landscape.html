Week 3 · Lesson 01 · AI Research

The AI research landscape.

"Just ask the AI" sounds simple — until you realise a plain chatbot, a web answer engine, a Deep Research agent and a document-grounded notebook are five very different animals with different costs, different access to live facts, and wildly different trust levels. This lesson is the honest map: what each category of AI research tool actually is, why they sometimes invent facts and fake citations, and how to pick the right one for the question in front of you.

01 / 05

Why AI research is a skill, not a button

Research used to mean opening a search engine, reading ten blue links, and stitching an answer together yourself. AI collapses that — but it also introduces a new risk: a tool that answers instantly and confidently, whether or not it actually looked anything up. Knowing which is which is the whole game.

Here is the distinction that trips up almost everyone. A plain chatbot answers from memory — patterns learned during training, frozen at some cutoff date. A research tool answers from sources — it goes out, retrieves real documents or web pages, and writes an answer grounded in them, ideally with links you can click. Same interface, a chat box, but a completely different relationship with the truth.

"AI research" is not one product and it is definitely not one button. It is a round trip: you ask a question, the tool either recalls from memory or retrieves from sources, and an answer comes back for you to verify. That last step never disappears. What you still own

Notice that in both runs, the answer returns to you. The grounded route retrieves real sources first, so the answer arrives with links you can open. The memory route skips the sources entirely and reconstructs an answer from training — fast, fluent, and occasionally invented. Neither route removes your job: read the answer, check the citations actually exist and actually say what the AI claims, then decide whether to trust it. AI research removes the ten-blue-links slog, not the judgment.

02 / 05

Five categories of research tool

"AI research tools" is a family, not a product. Five categories cover almost everything you will meet — and they differ most on the two axes that matter: do they see the live web, and can you trust their citations? Click each card to see cost, live-web access, citation quality, and the task it is actually built for.

The short version: plain chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) are brilliant reasoners with no live web by default. Web answer engines (Perplexity, ChatGPT search) search first and cite as they go. Deep Research agents spend minutes running dozens of searches to produce a long report. NotebookLM answers only from documents you upload. And academic tools (Elicit, Consensus) search real peer-reviewed papers. Same chat box, five very different jobs.

The two axes that decide everything

When someone asks "which AI should I use for research?", the answer is almost always determined by two questions. First: does it need to see the live web, or is the knowledge stable enough that training memory is fine? Second: how much does the citation have to be trustworthy — a casual explainer versus a claim going into a report your name is attached to. Plot any task on those two axes and the right category picks itself. Everything else — price, speed, interface — is secondary.

03 / 05

Why AI makes things up — and how grounding fixes it

The single most important thing to understand about AI research is why a chatbot will confidently give you a fake statistic or a citation to a paper that does not exist. It is not lying. It is doing exactly what it was built to do — and grounding is the fix. Step through it below.

A language model is, at heart, a very sophisticated next-word predictor. Ask it a question and it generates the most plausible-sounding continuation, one token at a time. When it knows the answer, plausible and true line up. But when it does not know — a niche fact, a specific citation, a recent event past its training cutoff — it does not stop and say "I don't know." It generates something that looks like an answer, because a fluent guess is statistically more likely than an admission of ignorance. That is a hallucination: confident, well-formed, and wrong.

Fabricated citations are the sharpest version of this. Asked for a source, the model produces a real-sounding author, a plausible journal, a believable year — a citation shaped correctly but pointing at nothing. Grounding — also called retrieval, or RAG — fixes this by changing the job. Instead of "recall the answer," the task becomes "here are real retrieved documents; answer using only these, and cite them." Grounding reduces, it does not erase

Grounding is the biggest single lever for trustworthy AI research — a grounded tool with real retrieved sources hallucinates far less than a plain chatbot answering from memory. But "less" is not "never." A grounded tool can still misread a source, quote it out of context, or cite a real page that does not actually support the claim. Retrieval fixes the fabrication problem; it does not remove your obligation to click the link and check that the source truly says what the AI claims it says.

Choose the right tool for the question

You do not memorise which tool to use — you ask a couple of questions about the task and the answer falls out. Walk the decision flow below: click the option that matches your question, and the right category, plus why, appears at the end.

The mistake beginners make is reaching for the same tool every time — usually a plain chatbot — and then being surprised when it invents a source or misses this week's news. Matching the tool to the question is a five-second habit that saves you from a wrong fact in a document. Start the flow and pick the branch that fits what you actually need.

The habit under the flow

Read the branches back and the pattern is simple: recent facts push you toward the live web; trusted claims push you toward grounded, cited tools; your own documents push you toward NotebookLM; and depth pushes you toward a Deep Research agent. A plain chatbot is a fine default for reasoning, brainstorming and explaining stable concepts — just not for facts that must be current or citations that must be real. Ask the two questions, pick the branch, then verify. That is the entire discipline.

05 / 05

Hype vs. reality: four claims, flipped

The internet promises "AI does your research for you." Before you paste an AI answer into anything that matters, flip each card and read what the claim looks like once you have actually leaned on these tools for real work. Honest expectations now save you from a fabricated citation in a report later.

None of this means "don't use AI for research." It means use it as leverage, not as an oracle. The realistic workflow is boring and reliable: pick the tool that fits the question, read what it retrieves, click through to the primary source, and confirm it says what the AI claims — then use it. AI makes each loop dramatically faster. It does not remove your responsibility for what you repeat as true.

A Deep Research agent produces an impressive, well-organised report — but it can still cite a weak source or over-claim a trend. Length is not rigour. Skim its sources, spot-check the key claims, and treat it as a strong first draft, not a final answer.

The golden rule

If you remember one thing from this lesson, make it this: verify against a primary source before you repeat an AI claim as fact. The primary source is the original — the actual paper, the official page, the real dataset — not another AI summary of it. Grounded tools make this easy by handing you the link; plain chatbots make it essential by sometimes not having one. The most productive AI researchers are not the ones who trust the output — they are the ones who use the time AI saves to click through and confirm.

What to carry forward

You now have the honest map of AI research. A plain chatbot answers from frozen memory; research tools answer from retrieved sources. Five categories cover the field — plain chatbots, web answer engines, Deep Research agents, NotebookLM, and academic tools — and they differ most on live-web access and citation trust. Models hallucinate because they predict plausible words, not verified truth; grounding (retrieval / RAG) reduces that sharply but never fully. Match the tool to the question, and always verify against the primary source. Next lesson: running your first grounded research query, step by step.
