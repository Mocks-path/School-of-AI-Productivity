Describe the document. Get the draft.

The blank page used to be the hardest part of writing. Now you describe what you need in plain English and AI hands you a first draft in seconds — a letter, a report, a summary, an outline. This lesson teaches the part that still belongs to you: how to ask well, run the draft loop, choose the right pattern, retune the tone and length, and catch the confident-sounding facts that aren't true.

01 / 06

Plain English in, a draft out

The old skill was staring down the blank page and forcing out a first sentence. The new skill is describing what you want clearly enough that AI can produce the draft — which you then shape. The second is far easier to learn, and it removes the part of writing everyone dreads.

Here is the shift. You used to open Word to an empty document and grind out a rough draft word by word. Now you type a sentence — "write a polite email declining a meeting invitation" — and AI writes the paragraph. But the sentence has to carry enough information: who it's for, what it needs to do, and what tone it should strike. Watch a vague request and a precise one produce very different drafts below.

The more your request pins down the audience, the purpose, and the tone, the less the AI has to guess — and the less you have to rewrite afterwards.

The lesson of the transformer is simple: the vague request wasn't wrong, it was under-specified, so the AI filled the gaps with a generic, one-size-fits-nobody draft. Precision in, precision out. Which brings us to what a good document request actually contains.

02 / 06

The anatomy of a good document prompt

A useful drafting request answers four questions before AI writes a word: who is this for, what should it achieve, how long and in what tone, and what real details must appear.

Think of it like briefing a colleague. "Write something about the project" gets you a shrug; "write a one-page status update for the client, upbeat but honest, covering the delay and the new timeline" gets you a usable draft. A document prompt works the same way. Skip these and the AI guesses — usually plausibly, sometimes off-key, and always in a voice that isn't quite yours.

Part 1

Audience

Who reads this — a client, your boss, a new hire, the whole company. AI writes very differently for a nervous first-time customer than for a senior executive. Name the reader.

Part 2

Purpose

What the document must actually do: inform, persuade, apologise, request, summarise. "Get them to approve the budget" shapes every sentence more than "write a memo" ever could.

Part 3

Tone & length

Formal or friendly, one paragraph or two pages, bullet points or prose. State it up front — "warm, under 150 words, no jargon" — or the AI picks a default that may not fit.

Part 4

The facts

The real names, dates, numbers, and details that must appear. AI cannot invent your project's true timeline — give it the facts, or it will fill the gap with plausible fiction.

Notice that none of these require you to be a polished writer. You supply the situation, the goal, and the true details; the AI supplies fluent prose. That division of labour — you own the intent and the facts, AI owns the wording — is the whole method of this lesson, and it repeats in every pattern below.

03 / 06

The draft loop: ask, read, refine, verify

A good document almost never arrives on the first try — and it isn't supposed to. AI writing is a conversation, not a vending machine. The skill is running the loop until the draft is genuinely yours.

Beginners ask once, get a mediocre draft, and either use it as-is or give up. The people who get real value treat the first draft as clay: they read it critically, tell the AI exactly what to change, and repeat — then verify the final result against reality. The loop's secret is that refining is where the quality lives. The first draft gets you 70% of the way in five seconds; the last 30% — the specific tone, your real details, the sentence that actually lands — comes from reading carefully and asking for precise changes. Verify last, and always: the loop ends with truth-checking, never before.

Five patterns cover almost every document

Writing tasks look endless, but most fall into a handful of shapes. Learn to recognise the shape, and you'll know exactly what to ask AI for.

You don't need to be a great writer to use these — that's what AI supplies. What's worth internalising is the name of the job, so that when a task lands on your desk you can think "this is a rewrite" or "this is a summary" and describe it cleanly. Every one of these is a sentence away. "Turn this rough email into something polite and professional" is a re-tone. "Give me the three key points from this five-page report" is a summary. "Draft this from the attached meeting notes" references your files. Name the job, describe the source and the goal — the wording is the AI's problem, not yours.

Retune tone and length on demand

The single most useful trick in AI writing: take one draft and instantly reshape it — more formal or more friendly, shorter or longer — without rewriting a word yourself.

You wrote a blunt one-liner to a colleague, but now it's going to a client and needs polish. Or a paragraph is perfect but twice as long as the space allows. Instead of manually rewording, you ask AI to shift the register. Move the two dials below and watch the same underlying message change its clothes — this is exactly what "make it warmer" or "cut it in half" does in practice.

The catch: it writes confidently, even when it's wrong

AI never sounds unsure. It will state a fake statistic, invent a quote, or cite a study that doesn't exist — all in the same fluent, authoritative voice it uses for the truth. Fluency is not accuracy.

This is the one habit that separates safe users from embarrassed ones. AI generates text that sounds right; it does not check whether the facts are real. The discipline that keeps you safe: verify before you send

An AI draft is a strong start, not a finished document. Every real name, number, quote, and citation is a place where the model may have confidently made something up. Before this leaves your outbox, run it past a short, non-negotiable check — the same way you'd proofread a letter that carries your name.

The point isn't distrust — it's that a document which reads well and a document which is true are two different things, and only your check tells them apart. AI removes the tedium of the blank page and the first draft. It does not remove your responsibility for what goes out under your name. Draft fast, verify carefully, then send.

What to carry forward

You don't fight the blank page anymore — you describe what you need and let AI supply the prose. But describing well, running the draft loop, recognising the pattern, retuning tone and length, and verifying every fact before you send are skills that stay firmly yours. That's where the value moved.
