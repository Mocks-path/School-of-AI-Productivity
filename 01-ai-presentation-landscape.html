Week 4 · Lesson 01 · AI Presentations

The AI presentation landscape.

A blank slide is one of the most intimidating things in office life. You have ideas — but turning them into a clean, structured, good-looking deck used to eat an entire afternoon. AI changes the starting point: you describe the presentation you want, and a tool drafts a full deck in seconds. This lesson is your honest map of that landscape — what "AI for slides" really means today, which tools do what, how a deck generator actually works, and where you still have to take over the story.

01 / 04

Why AI for slides matters

Presentations are how ideas travel through an organisation — pitches, updates, training, reports. Yet most people don't struggle with the idea; they struggle with the blank canvas: what goes on each slide, in what order, and how to make it look intentional rather than thrown together. AI attacks exactly that friction.

Before AI, building a deck meant three separate jobs done by hand: structuring (deciding the slides and their order), writing (the words on each slide), and designing (layout, colour, spacing, alignment). Each is a skill of its own, and doing all three at once is why decks take so long and so often end up as walls of bullet points. The gap between "I know what I want to say" and "here's a finished slide" is where the hours vanished.

"AI for slides" means closing that gap. You describe the presentation — a topic, an audience, maybe a document to draw from — and an AI generator drafts the whole structure, the slide text, and a coherent visual theme in one pass. It's not magic and it's not final: think of it as a fast first draft of an entire deck that you then direct and refine. The tool proposes; you decide the story. What you still own

Notice where the sequence ends: the draft comes back to you. AI handles structure, first-pass wording and a consistent look — the mechanical heavy lifting. What it can't do is know why this deck matters, which single point the audience must remember, or the story that connects the slides. That judgment stays yours. The whole skill of AI presentations is learning to hand off the drafting and keep the direction.

02 / 04

The tool landscape, compared

"AI for slides" isn't one product. It's a growing field of tools with very different starting points, design quality and — critically — how faithfully they export to a real PowerPoint file. Confusing them is the most common beginner mistake. Click each tool to see its generation mode, design quality, editability, PPTX export fidelity, cost and best fit.

Broadly there are two camps. Some tools live inside the apps you already use — Copilot in PowerPoint, PowerPoint Designer, and Google Slides with Gemini — so the output is a native editable file from the start. Others are standalone AI-first generators — Gamma, Beautiful.ai, Canva — that produce beautiful decks quickly but in their own environment, where exporting to .pptx can lose some polish. Same goal, very different trade-offs. Click a card to compare.

The line that matters most: export fidelity

There's one dividing question that beginners always miss: does the tool produce a real PowerPoint, or its own format that you export later? Copilot, Designer and Google Slides give you a native editable file from the first second. Gamma, Beautiful.ai and Canva build in their own beautiful environment — and when you download a .pptx, fonts, spacing and smart layouts can shift or flatten into images. If your workflow requires handing off an editable PowerPoint to a colleague, that export step is where the polish quietly leaks. Always test the export early, not the night before the meeting.

03 / 04

Prompt-to-deck vs document-to-deck

However you generate a deck, you feed the AI one of two things: a short prompt describing what you want, or an existing document to transform into slides. They feel similar but behave very differently — and picking the wrong one is why some AI decks come out thin and others come out cluttered. Click each mode to see how it works and when to reach for it.

The honest framing for this whole week: an AI deck generator is a fast, tireless designer who has seen every presentation ever made but has never sat in your meeting. From a prompt it invents plausible content — great for brainstorming, risky on facts. From a document it reorganises what you already wrote — faithful to your material, but only as good as the source. The rule of thumb

If you're exploring an idea and don't have material yet, prompt-to-deck gives you a scaffold to react to — just verify every fact it invents. If you already have the content — a report, a doc, meeting notes — document-to-deck keeps you faithful to your own material and saves you from AI making things up. Most professionals end up hybrid: draft the structure from a prompt, then paste in your real numbers and story. Whichever you choose, the AI produces the draft; you own what's true.

04 / 04

The honest limitations, flipped

The internet promises that AI will "make your whole presentation for you." Before you lean on it in front of an audience, flip each card and read what the promise looks like once you've actually shipped a few AI decks. Honest expectations now save you from a generic slide — or a wrong fact — in a real meeting later.

None of this means "don't use AI for slides." It means use it as a drafting engine, not an oracle. The realistic workflow is calm and repeatable: describe the deck, let AI structure and design a first pass, then cut the fluff, verify every claim, and layer in the one story only you can tell. That last part is the principle behind this entire week — AI drafts, you direct the story.

Reality

By default AI decks are generic and wordy — plausible bullet points that could belong to anyone. They're a strong first draft, not a finished talk. Cut half the text, sharpen every title, and add the specific example only you know. AI removes the blank page, not the editing.

Reality

Prompt-to-deck tools can hallucinate — invent a statistic, a quote, a market size or a date that reads perfectly but isn't real. Confidence is not accuracy. Verify every number and named fact against a source before it lands in front of an audience.

Reality

The opposite. You still need to judge whether a slide is clear, whether the order tells a story, and what to cut. AI rewards people who understand presentations — it just removes the tedious layout work. Taste and story are exactly what it can't supply.

Your getting-started path

Start small and safe. First, pick one tool that matches your workflow — if colleagues need an editable file, start with Copilot or Google Slides; if you want beautiful fast drafts, try Gamma or Canva. Second, generate a short deck from a clear prompt and notice what's generic. Third, try document-to-deck by feeding in a real doc, and compare. Always, cut the fluff, verify every fact, and export a test file before you rely on it. Do that a few times and AI becomes a genuine part of how you present.
