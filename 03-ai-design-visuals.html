Week 4 · Lesson 03 · AI PowerPoint

A slide is a picture of an idea, not a page of text.

AI can fill a deck in minutes — with walls of text, off-brand clip-art, and charts that quietly lie. Design is what separates a slide people read from a slide people ignore. In this lesson you learn the visual principles that actually move an audience, then wield AI as a design assistant: on-brand themes, honest charts, generated imagery done right — and the human judgement that keeps all of it true.

01 / 05

Good design is four decisions, not a talent

Beginners think "good-looking slides" is a gift some people have. It isn't. Designers run every slide through the same handful of principles — hierarchy, whitespace, contrast, and less-text-more-visual. Learn the checklist and your slides stop looking amateur, whether you or an AI built them.

The mistake is treating the slide as a document — cramming every sentence you might say onto the screen. A slide is a visual; your voice is the document. So each slide gets one job. First you set hierarchy so the eye knows what matters most, then you add whitespace so that thing can breathe, then you use contrast to separate signal from noise, and finally you replace text with a visual wherever a picture would say it faster. Why "one job per slide" is the whole game

Each principle serves a single idea: the slide has one point, and everything on it either advances that point or gets cut. Hierarchy names the point, whitespace protects it, contrast highlights it, and a visual delivers it faster than a sentence can. When AI drafts your deck, it will happily ignore all four — so these principles are also your edit checklist for anything the machine hands back.

02 / 05

A vague image prompt gets vague, off-brand art

"Make me an image for this slide" returns generic stock-looking sludge. Generated imagery is only as good as the description you feed it. A strong prompt names the subject, the style, the mood, the composition, and the colours — so the output fits your brand instead of fighting it.

Think of a prompt like a creative brief you'd hand a designer. Say what the picture shows, how it should look (flat illustration, photo, line icon), the feeling (calm, energetic, premium), the framing (wide, centred, negative space on the left for text), and the palette (your brand's warm ochre and cream). Turn the first result into the next prompt

Image generation is iterative, not one-shot. When the AI returns something close but too busy, your next prompt writes itself: "same illustration, flat style, more negative space on the left, remove the extra characters." Each pass tightens the fit to your slide. Beginners accept the first image; designers run five prompts and pick the one that leaves room for the headline.

03 / 05

On-brand beats impressive — and decorative-but-empty is worse than nothing

The prettiest AI image in the world is a mistake if it clashes with your brand or says nothing. Two failures dominate generated visuals: off-brand (wrong colours, wrong style, obvious stock feel) and decorative-but-empty (a slick picture that adds zero meaning). A good visual is on-palette and it earns its place by making a point.

This is where a brand kit pays off. When your theme, fonts and colours live in one place — PowerPoint's Slide Master, a saved template, or a brand-kit tool — every generated element can be nudged to match. A licensing note you cannot skip

On-brand or not, check that you're allowed to use the image. AI-generated visuals raise real questions: some tools grant you commercial rights, others don't, and a model may reproduce a copyrighted character or a recognisable logo you're not licensed to use. Read the tool's terms, avoid prompts that name living artists or brands, and for anything client-facing keep a note of where each asset came from. "The AI made it" is not, on its own, a licence.

04 / 05

The right chart tells the truth faster

A chart on a slide is an argument, and the wrong chart type either hides your point or — worse — misleads the room. Before AI draws anything, name the question: comparison, trend over time, composition, or relationship. The question chooses the chart; the chart must be honest.

Four questions cover most decks. Comparing categories? Bar chart. Showing change over time? Line chart. Showing parts of a whole? Pie — only with a handful of slices. Relating two numbers or spotting an odd one out? Scatter plot. AI can draft any of these from a plain-English request, but you decide what it argues. Verify the numbers before the slide leaves the room

AI will draw a beautiful chart from whatever data you paste — including data it read wrong. It can truncate a y-axis to exaggerate a difference, plot the wrong column, or invent a total that never appears in your sheet. A chart looks authoritative either way. The non-negotiable step is checking that the picture tells the same story as the numbers. Use the sanity-check below to catch the classic tricks.

From wall-of-text to a slide people actually read

The most common AI-deck failure is the wall-of-text slide: a paragraph the AI dumped onto a canvas because you asked it to "put the content on a slide". Redesign is a skill you can run every time — pull out the one point, turn support into icons or a number, and delete the rest. The move is always the same: find the single sentence that is the point, promote it to a headline, convert three supporting facts into a row of icons or a big number, and cut everything the audience will hear you say out loud. Click Redesign to transform the wall of text into a clean visual slide.

The three-second honesty check

You don't need to redraw the chart — you need to ask three questions. Does the y-axis start at zero (or is a small change made to look huge)? Do the pie slices actually sum to 100%? Does the shape match the data, not a story you wanted to tell? If a chart fails the gut check, AI probably read a column wrong or "helpfully" flattered the trend. On slides, verify first — then present.

What to carry forward

You now know that a great slide is designed, not decorated. Hierarchy, whitespace, contrast and less-text-more-visual turn a page of words into a picture of an idea. AI accelerates every step — theme, imagery, charts — and just as easily produces off-brand art and dishonest charts, which is why the human owns the final look and the final truth.

  • Run every slide through four principles: hierarchy → whitespace → contrast → visual
  • One job per slide; your voice is the document, the slide is the picture
  • A strong image prompt names subject, style, mood, composition and brand palette
  • On-brand beats impressive; decorative-but-empty is worse than no image at all
  • Check licensing — "the AI made it" is not automatically a right to use it
  • Let Designer and Copilot draft layouts, but override when they miss the point
  • The question chooses the chart; check the axis, the slices and the totals for honesty
