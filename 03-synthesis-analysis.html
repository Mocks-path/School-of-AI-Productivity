Week 3 · Lesson 03 · AI Research

From twelve sources to one honest answer.

Gathering sources is the easy part — anyone can open twenty tabs. Synthesis is the hard part: reading across all of them, finding where they agree, where they clash, and what they actually add up to. In this lesson you turn a pile of sources into a grounded, cited answer through a repeatable pipeline — and learn to catch the moment AI smooths real disagreement into a comfortable, false consensus.

01 / 05

Synthesis is a pipeline, not a summary

Beginners paste ten articles into a chatbot and ask "summarise this." What comes back is an average — smooth, plausible, and blind to the one source that disagreed. Real synthesis runs every set of sources through the same six steps, and AI can help at each one. Master the pipeline and "insight" stops being a lucky blend.

The mistake is collapsing everything into one summary in a single shot. Skip the middle and you get a confident paragraph that hides its own contradictions. The pipeline forces order: first you collect the sources, then you ground the AI in them (so it can only speak from what you gave it), then you extract the claims from each, compare them side by side, synthesize the agreements and tensions, and finally verify every line traces back to a real passage. Tip — grounding comes before extracting for a reason. An ungrounded model fills gaps with its training data; a grounded one can only speak from your sources.

Why order matters

Each stage protects the next. Grounding protects extraction; extraction protects the comparison; the comparison protects the synthesis. Break the chain — synthesize before you've extracted each source's actual claims — and the AI invents a tidy consensus that no single source ever made. The smoother the summary, the more it may have paved over.

02 / 05

Every level of summary keeps something and drops something

A summary is a trade. Compress a 2,000-word report into one line and you gain speed but lose every caveat, condition, and number that made it true. There's no "correct" length — only the right level for the decision you're making. The skill is knowing what each level throws away.

Watch the same source shrink across three levels. The full passage keeps everything — the finding, the sample size, the caveat, the exception. Key points keeps the claims but starts shedding conditions. The one-liner keeps only the headline — fast to quote, dangerous to trust, because the "but only when…" is gone. Toggle each level and read the meter: how much fidelity survives, and exactly what got dropped.

Match the level to the stakes

For a headline in a slide, the one-liner is fine. For a decision that costs money or credibility, drop back to key points — or the full passage — and read the caveat you were about to summarise away. When you ask AI to compress, ask it in the same breath: "what did you leave out, and would any of it change the conclusion?"

03 / 05

A synthesis matrix makes agreement and conflict visible

The moment you have more than three sources, you can't hold them in your head. A synthesis matrix — sources down the side, themes across the top — turns "I read a lot" into a map you can actually reason about. Where a column fills up, you've found consensus; where it's sparse or split, you've found the real story.

Here's the whole idea: each row is a source, each column is a theme or claim, and each cell records what that source says about that theme — agrees, disagrees, or silent. Fill the grid and patterns leap out: a theme every source hits is settled; a theme where one source breaks ranks is where you should dig. Click cells to log each source's position, then read the synthesis it builds below.

The empty and split cells are the point

Beginners chase the full columns — the things everyone agrees on — because they feel safe. But a theme where four sources agree and one sharply disagrees is far more interesting than one where all five nod along. The matrix earns its keep by making the outlier impossible to overlook, instead of letting AI quietly average it into the consensus.

04 / 05

A grounded answer shows its work

The single biggest upgrade in AI research is grounding: tools like NotebookLM answer only from the sources you upload, and attach a citation to every claim that jumps you straight back to the exact passage. No citation means no evidence — and that's a feature, not a limitation.

An ungrounded chatbot blends your sources with everything it ever read and gives you a fluent answer you can't check. A grounded notebook does the opposite: it quotes your material, footnotes each sentence, and lets you click a citation to see the sentence it came from — in context, highlighted. If a claim has no citation, treat it as the model guessing. Click each citation below to reveal the passage it's standing on.

No citation, no claim

Grounding turns "trust me" into "check me." Make it a rule: any sentence in an AI synthesis that doesn't trace to a source is a hypothesis, not a finding. NotebookLM enforces this by design; when you use an ungrounded tool, enforce it yourself by asking "which source says that, and quote the line."

05 / 05

Genuine consensus vs. AI's false consensus

AI is built to be agreeable and fluent — which makes it dangerously good at manufacturing consensus that isn't there. It rounds off the sharp disagreement, drops the lone dissenting study, and hands you a smooth "experts agree" paragraph. Real synthesis has to tell the difference between sources that actually agree and an AI that's just smoothing.

Genuine consensus is when independent sources, using different methods, land in the same place — that's signal worth trusting. False consensus is when the AI's summary reads as agreement but the underlying sources were mixed, and the disagreement got averaged out of existence. The tell is always in the sources, not the summary. Click each scenario to see which one it is — and how to preserve the nuance the AI wanted to erase.

Force the disagreement back into view

The fix is a prompt habit: never ask only "what do the sources say?" Ask "where do these sources disagree, and which one is the outlier?" A grounded tool will surface the split; an over-smoothing one will suddenly get vaguer — and that vagueness is your signal that the tidy consensus was manufactured. Keep the dissent; it's usually where the truth is hiding.

What to carry forward

You now know that synthesis is manufactured, not summarised. A pile of sources becomes a trustworthy answer only when it's collected, grounded, extracted, compared, synthesised, and verified back to real passages. AI accelerates every step — and quietly smooths away disagreement at every step too, which is why the human owns the tension the machine wants to erase.

  • Run every source set through the pipeline: collect → ground → extract → compare → synthesize → verify
  • Ground the model first — an ungrounded AI fills gaps with training data, not your sources
  • Every level of summary trades speed for fidelity; ask what each one dropped
  • A synthesis matrix makes agreement and — more importantly — the outliers visible
  • No citation, no claim: any ungrounded sentence is a hypothesis, not a finding
  • Distrust smooth consensus — ask where the sources disagree and which one is the outlier
