Week 3 · Lesson 04 · AI Research

Trust nothing until you've checked it.

You've learned to prompt, search, and synthesise with AI. This lesson is the discipline that makes all of it safe to publish: verifying claims, confirming citations are real, judging whether a source deserves your trust, quoting people honestly, and knowing when to disclose that AI helped. It is the least glamorous week of the course — and the one that keeps your name clean. Let's be honest about where AI lies, and build the habits that catch it.

01 / 05

A claim isn't true because it sounds true

AI writes with total confidence whether it's right or wrong — the fluent sentence and the fabricated one look identical. So you can't verify by feel. You need a repeatable pipeline that treats every factual claim as guilty until a primary source proves it innocent. Run the same five steps, every time, and hallucinations stop slipping through.

The pipeline moves from cheap checks to expensive ones. First you flag the specific claims worth checking — names, numbers, dates, quotes, "studies show." Then you rank them by risk: a wrong birth year is embarrassing; a wrong drug dosage is dangerous. High-risk claims earn the effort of tracing back to a primary source — the original paper, the actual filing, the person who said it — not a summary of a summary. Then you trace the citation to confirm the source actually says what you're claiming. Only then do you decide: keep it, fix it, or cut it.

Watch it assemble. Each stage is something you can do in minutes; skipping the last three is how confident nonsense reaches your reader.

Is this citation real — or invented?

The most dangerous AI failure isn't a vague answer; it's a crisp, formatted, authoritative-looking citation for a paper that does not exist. Real author names, a plausible journal, a DOI that looks right — all confidently assembled from thin air. Here's how to test one. Pick a citation below and run the four checks a fabrication can't survive.

The tests, from fastest to slowest: does the DOI resolve to a real page? Do the title, authors, and journal match that page exactly? And does the paper's content actually support the claim you're attaching to it? A fabricated citation fails one of these — usually the first. A misused real citation passes the first three and fails the last, which is why the content check is the one people skip and shouldn't.

What separates fake from misused

A fabricated citation collapses at the DOI or the index — the paper simply isn't there. A misused citation is scarier because it's real: correct authors, correct journal, correct year — attached to a claim the paper never makes. Both fail your standard, but only the content check catches the second. Never cite a source you haven't opened and read the relevant lines of.

03 / 05

Does this source deserve your trust?

A citation can be perfectly real and still be a bad source to lean on. Verification isn't only "does it exist" — it's "how much weight can this bear." Judge a source on five dimensions: authority (who wrote it and do they know?), evidence (data or assertion?), bias (who benefits from you believing it?), recency (is it still current?), and corroboration (does anyone independent agree?).

No single dimension is decisive. A recent, well-evidenced blog from an anonymous author is worth more than a stale, unsourced page from a famous institution. The evaluator below blends the strong dimensions and lets weak ones — especially missing evidence or heavy bias — drag the score down, the way a careful reader's judgement should. Quote honestly, disclose plainly

Two ethics habits separate careful researchers from careless ones: attributing quotes and paraphrases correctly, and being upfront about when AI helped. Neither is complicated. Both are constantly botched — usually not from malice but from moving fast and letting AI reword a source until the attribution quietly drifts from the truth.

When & how to disclose AI use

Transparency, sized to the stakes

A
Disclose when it shaped the substance. If AI drafted arguments, generated analysis, or wrote passages a reader would attribute to you, say so.
B
Follow the venue's rule first. Journals, universities, and employers have explicit AI policies — the correct disclosure is whatever they require, in their format.
C
Be specific, not vague. "AI assisted with drafting and code" beats a coy "AI may have been used." Name the tool and the role it played.
D
Light touch, light note. Grammar fixes or brainstorming rarely need a formal statement — but never hide substantive help behind "just editing."

The test that settles most cases

When unsure whether to disclose, ask: would a reasonable reader feel misled if they learned how much AI was involved? If yes, disclose. The goal isn't confession for its own sake — it's making sure credit and responsibility land where they belong. You own everything you publish under your name, including the parts a machine wrote for you.

The uncomfortable truth about AI detectors

A lot of people hope a "detector" will do this work for them — paste text, get a verdict, done. It won't. AI-writing detectors are unreliable in both directions, and leaning on them causes real harm. This is the honest panel: what they get wrong, and why accountability can't be outsourced to a tool.

Detectors fail two ways at once. They flag genuine human writing as AI — false positives that have wrongly accused students and writers, hitting non-native English speakers hardest because their phrasing reads as "too regular." And they miss lightly-edited AI text — false negatives that sail straight through. A verdict that's confidently wrong in both directions isn't evidence; it's a coin flip with a percentage sign.

Figure 5 · Why detectors can't be your judge
✗ False positives
Humanflagged as AI

Real essays — often by non-native English speakers or plain, structured writers — get labelled machine-made. Careers and grades have been damaged by a number that was simply wrong.

✗ False negatives
AIpasses as human

A few paraphrase passes or a "humaniser" tool, and AI-generated text scores clean. Anyone actually trying to cheat can beat the detector in minutes.

The accountability rule: a detector cannot verify a fact, confirm a citation, or judge a source — and it cannot be trusted to tell human from machine. So it decides nothing that matters. What matters is the same as it has always been: you checked the claims, you read the sources, and you stand behind what you published. Integrity is a process you run, not a score you paste.

Before you publish anything, run this

Everything this week compresses into one final checklist — the pass you make before your name goes on the work. Tick each one honestly; it's the difference between "I think it's right" and "I checked."

Figure 7 · The publish-with-integrity checklist
✓

Every claimed fact traced to a primary source

No number, name, or "studies show" survives without you opening the original and confirming it there — never the AI's recollection.

✓

Every citation opened, not just formatted

DOI resolves, metadata matches, and the paper actually says what you claim. A tidy reference list means nothing unread.

✓

Sources weighed, not just counted

Authority, evidence, bias, recency, corroboration considered. Self-interested single sources treated as leads, not facts.

✓

Quotes and paraphrases attributed correctly

Verbatim text in quotation marks, borrowed ideas credited even when reworded, no citation quietly dropped.

✓

AI use disclosed where it shaped the substance

Follow the venue's policy, be specific about the tool and its role, and never hide meaningful help behind "just editing."

✓

You own it — no detector, no excuse

You verified, you read, you stand behind it. Accountability is yours whether or not a machine drafted a word.

Why this is the habit that lasts

Tools will change; models will get better and worse in surprising ways. The discipline in this checklist won't age, because it doesn't depend on any tool being trustworthy — it depends on you being the last line of verification. That's the whole point of the week, and the whole point of the course: AI makes you faster, but it never makes you right. You do that.

What to carry forward

AI writes fiction and fact in the same confident voice, so you verify by process, not by feel. Trace every checkable claim to a primary source. Test citations for real — DOI, metadata, and above all content. Weigh sources on authority, evidence, bias, recency, and corroboration instead of counting them. Attribute honestly and disclose AI use where it shaped the work. And never hand your judgement to a detector — the accountability was always, and only, yours.

  • Fact-check pipeline: flag → rank by risk → primary source → trace citation → decide
  • Test citations: DOI resolves · metadata matches · content actually supports the claim
  • Judge sources on authority × evidence × bias × recency × corroboration — not headcount
  • Quote verbatim in marks, credit reworded ideas, disclose AI where it shaped substance
  • Detectors are wrong both ways — you verified it, you read it, you own it
