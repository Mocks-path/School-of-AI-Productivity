From a raw table to a real insight.

A spreadsheet full of numbers is not an answer — it's raw material. In this lesson you turn a messy table into insight through a repeatable pipeline: clean it, structure it, ask it a sharp question, visualize the answer, and — the step everyone skips — validate what the AI hands back before you trust it.

01 / 05

Insight is a pipeline, not a lucky glance

Beginners stare at a wall of rows hoping a pattern will jump out. It rarely does. Analysts don't have better eyes — they run every dataset through the same five steps, and AI can help at each one. Master the pipeline and the "insight" stops being luck.

The mistake is jumping straight from a raw export to a chart. Skip the middle and you get a beautiful graph of dirty data — confident, colourful, and wrong. The pipeline forces order: first you clean the obvious mess, then you structure the range as a proper Table so Excel and AI understand it, then you ask one specific question, then you visualize only that answer, and finally you validate the result against something you already know. Tip — the order is not optional. A chart built on unclean data just makes the error look official.

Why order matters

Each stage protects the next. Cleaning protects structuring; structuring protects the question; the question protects the chart. Break the chain anywhere and the error flows downstream, growing more convincing at every step. A pretty dashboard is the most dangerous place for a mistake to hide — nobody double-checks something that looks finished.

02 / 05

A vague question gets a vague answer

AI is only as sharp as the question you feed it. "Analyse this data" returns a shrug dressed up as a summary. "Which region's revenue fell the most from Q1 to Q2, and by what percent?" returns a number you can act on. The skill isn't the tool — it's the question.

Good analytical questions share three traits: they name a metric (revenue, count, average), a dimension to slice by (region, month, product), and a comparison or time frame (versus last quarter, top 5, since March). Miss any one and the AI has to guess — and it guesses confidently. Click a vague question on the left and watch its sharpened twin on the right.

Turn the AI's answer into the next question

Analysis is a conversation, not a single shot. When the AI tells you the North region fell 18%, your next question writes itself: "Was that one product or all of them?" Each answer narrows the next question until you reach the actual cause. Beginners ask one big question and stop; analysts ask ten small ones and arrive somewhere.

03 / 05

The PivotTable, finally explained

A PivotTable is the single most useful thing in Excel that beginners avoid. It sounds technical; it's actually just four buckets. Drop a field into Rows, Columns, or Values, add a Filter, and Excel summarizes thousands of rows in an instant.

Here's the whole idea: Rows are the categories you list down the side. Columns split each row across the top. Values are the numbers being crunched — a sum, a count, an average. Filters hide rows you don't want to count. That's it. Assign the fields below and watch the summary rebuild itself. 04 / 05

The right chart answers the question you're asking

A chart is not decoration — it's an argument. The wrong chart type hides the very pattern you were hunting for. Before you pick a shape, name your question: comparison, trend over time, composition, or relationship. The question chooses the chart.

Four questions cover most of what a beginner needs. Comparing categories? Bar chart. Watching something change over time? Line chart. Showing parts of a whole? Pie — but only if you have a handful of slices. Looking for a relationship between two numbers? Scatter plot. Outliers, meanwhile, show up best on scatter and box plots. Spotting trends and outliers

A trend is a direction that holds across many points — best read on a line chart, where your eye follows the slope. An outlier is a single point that refuses to behave — best caught on a scatter or by sorting the raw column. AI is good at flagging both ("March spikes 3× the average"), but always ask why before you celebrate: an outlier is often a data-entry error, not a discovery.

05 / 05

Never ship a finding you haven't sanity-checked

AI reads your spreadsheet faster than you can — and misreads it just as fast. It can sum the wrong column, mistake text dates for numbers, or count a header row as data. The output looks authoritative either way. The final, non-negotiable step is validation.

You can "ask your data a question" two ways, and the choice has a privacy dimension. Copilot inside Excel works on the file already open, under your organisation's data protections — nothing leaves your tenant. Uploading a CSV to ChatGPT or Claude is powerful and flexible, but you are sending that data to an external service — so strip names, emails and anything confidential first, and check your company's policy before uploading customer data. The three-second sanity check

You don't need to re-do the AI's maths — you need one number you already trust to compare against. Does the total roughly match last year? Does the date range make sense (there is no month 13)? Is the average in a believable range? If a finding fails the gut check, the AI probably misread a column. Trust, but verify — and with AI, verify first.

What to carry forward

You now know that insight is manufactured, not spotted. Raw data becomes an answer only when it's cleaned, structured, questioned sharply, visualized honestly, and checked against reality. AI accelerates every step — and quietly introduces errors at every step too, which is why the human owns the last word.

  • Run every dataset through the pipeline: clean → structure → ask → visualize → validate
  • A sharp question names a metric, a dimension, and a time frame
  • PivotTables are four buckets — Rows, Columns, Values, Filters — nothing more
  • The question chooses the chart; the wrong chart hides the pattern
  • Copilot keeps data in-house; uploading a CSV sends it out — strip the sensitive columns
  • AI misreads columns and dates — sanity-check every finding before you ship it
