Week 3 · Lesson 02 · AI Research

Ask well. Read the sources. Trust nothing blindly.

Answer engines like Perplexity and ChatGPT search will hand you a fluent paragraph with footnotes in seconds. That speed is the trap. This lesson teaches the part that still belongs to you: turning a vague question into a strong research prompt, running the search loop that reaches primary sources, reading a citation critically, and catching the confident-sounding answer that happens to be wrong.

01 / 06

Vague question in, weak research out

A search tool answers the question you actually typed — not the one in your head. The old skill was choosing keywords. The new skill is describing what you need precisely enough that the machine gathers the right sources instead of the loudest ones.

Here is the shift. You used to type three keywords into a search box and skim ten blue links yourself. Now you type a sentence and an answer engine reads the pages for you and writes a summary with citations. But that summary is only as good as the question. A thin question — "best CRM?" — invites a thin, generic, possibly outdated answer stitched from whoever ranked highest. A question that carries your context, your constraints, and a demand for sources steers the tool toward evidence you can actually act on. 02 / 06

The anatomy of a strong research prompt

A useful research request answers four questions before the engine ever searches: what exactly are you asking, who's asking and why, what would make an answer wrong for you, and what evidence you'll accept.

Think of it as briefing a research assistant. "Look into CRMs" gets you a Wikipedia-flavoured shrug; "find me the two best CRMs for a 4-person agency under $50/user, with pricing pages linked, ignore anything older than a year" gets you something you can decide on. A research prompt works the same way. Skip these and the engine fills the gaps with the internet's defaults — popular, generic, and often stale.

Part 1

Specificity

The exact thing you want to know, narrowed to the real decision: not "AI tools" but "note-taking apps that transcribe meetings and export to Notion." A sharp question retrieves sharp sources.

Part 2

Context

Who you are and why you're asking — team size, budget, region, skill level, what you've already ruled out. The engine can't see your situation, so it answers for the average one unless you say.

Part 3

Constraints

The boundaries that make an answer usable: a price ceiling, a date cutoff ("nothing before 2024"), a platform, a regulation. Constraints are what turn a list of options into a shortlist.

Part 4

Ask for sources

Explicitly demand citations, publication dates, and primary sources — "link the official pricing page, note when each source was published." This is what lets you verify instead of trust.

Notice that none of these require you to know clever search operators. You describe the decision and the evidence you'll accept; the engine does the retrieval. That division of labour — you own the question and the judgement, the engine owns the reading-at-scale — is the whole method of this lesson, and it repeats in every pattern below.

03 / 06

The search loop: ask, read, follow, capture, verify

Good research is a loop, not a single query. You ask, read what comes back, follow the citations down to the primary source, capture what's worth keeping, and verify the claim before you rely on it — then loop again on what's still fuzzy.

The failure mode is stopping at step one: reading the engine's summary and treating it as the answer. That summary is a starting point — a map of where the evidence lives, not the evidence itself. The value is in the loop. Click through each stage to see what it asks of you and how to do it well.

04 / 06

Four patterns cover almost every search job

Research tasks look endless, but most fall into a handful of shapes. Learn to recognise the shape and you'll know which tool to reach for and how to frame the ask.

You don't need to memorise the tools — they change every few months. What's worth internalising is the name of the job, so that when a question lands you can think "this is a quick answer-engine lookup" or "this deserves a Deep Research task" and frame it cleanly. Every one of these is a framing away. "Give me a quick, cited overview of X, note the dates" is an answer-engine lookup. "Compare five options against my constraints, cite official pages, flag disagreements between sources" is a Deep Research task. Name the job, hand it the right tool, and — always — read the sources it returns rather than the summary alone.

05 / 06

Reading a citation critically

A footnote is not proof. An answer engine can cite a forum post, a two-year-old blog, or a vendor's own marketing page as confidently as it cites a peer-reviewed study. The skill is opening the citation and judging it — is this source credible, current, and actually about what the sentence claimed.

Here's a real hazard. The engine writes a clean sentence — "Studies show X" — and links a source. You nod and move on. But when you click through, the "study" is a company blog with no data, published three years ago, that never actually said X. The footnote created false confidence. A footnote tells you where a claim came from, not whether it's true. Credible, current, relevant, and independent — a citation that misses any of those is a lead to chase, not a fact to trust.

06 / 06

Spot the confident wrong answer

The most dangerous output isn't the one that says "I'm not sure." It's the one that's fluent, specific, and completely wrong — a made-up statistic, an outdated price quoted as current, a citation to a page that doesn't say what it claims. AI reads the situation for you, but the confidence is an interface, not a guarantee.

Beginners treat a confident tone as a correctness signal. It isn't. Answer engines can quote stale numbers, invent citations, average away real disagreement, and state a plausible-but-false claim with total poise. The discipline that keeps you honest: verify before you cite

Answer engines are usually right, but "usually" is not "always" — and a fabricated statistic pasted into a client deck is your name on someone else's mistake. Never forward a claim on the strength of its tone. Before you rely on anything an engine tells you, run it through this six-point check.

The point isn't paranoia — it's that an answer which sounds authoritative and an answer which is correct are two different things, and only opening the source tells them apart. AI removes the tedium of reading a hundred pages. It does not remove your responsibility for the claim. Verify small, then cite with confidence — the real kind, earned by looking.

What to carry forward

You don't hunt through blue links anymore — you brief an engine and it reads at scale. But framing the question, running the loop to the primary source, interrogating a citation, catching the confident error, and verifying before you cite are skills that stay firmly yours. That's where the value moved.
