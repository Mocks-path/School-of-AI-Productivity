The edit pass — before & after

AI writes slides that look finished but read like a first draft: too many words, hedged phrasing, claims it can't back up. The polish pass is where a good deck becomes a great one. The rule is simple — a slide is a headline your audience reads in three seconds, not a paragraph they squint at while you talk over it. Here is the same slide before and after one honest edit pass.

✗ Before — AI's first draft
Our Q3 Performance Results and Key Takeaways
In the third quarter of this year, our team was able to achieve a significant increase in overall revenue, growing by approximately 24% when compared to the same period in the previous year, which was largely driven by strong performance across all of our major regional markets and product categories.

Full sentences. A title that says nothing. A number buried in a wall of hedging ("approximately", "largely"). The audience reads instead of listening to you.

✓ After — one edit pass
Q3 revenue up 24% year-over-year
• Growth across every region
• Led by [Product X] and [Region Y]
• Verified against the finance close

Headline states the point. Bullets are scannable. The hedge words are gone, and the number is flagged as verified. The detail lives in your mouth, not on the slide.

The five moves of every polish pass, in order: cut words (headline + bullets, not sentences) → fix flow (does slide 4 follow from slide 3?) → verify facts (every number AI wrote is a number you must be able to defend) → generate speaker notes (the detail, off the slide) → rehearse out loud. You'll practise each below.

    Speaker Coach & Presenter View — the tools that help you rehearse

    PowerPoint has two features made for this last mile. Use them as instruments, not verdicts.

    Speaker Coach

    What it does: you rehearse into your mic and it gives private feedback — pace (too fast when nervous), filler words ("um", "basically", "so"), monotone, and reading straight off your slides.

    How to hold it: it's a mirror, not a judge. Its counts are guidance, not a grade — a few "ums" are human, and passion sometimes reads as "too fast." Use it to notice patterns, then decide what actually matters for your room.

    Presenter View

    What it does: on your screen you see the current slide, the next slide, your speaker notes, and a timer — while the audience sees only the clean slide.

    How to hold it: this is why your notes belong in the notes pane, not on the slide. Rehearse with Presenter View at least once so it isn't a surprise live. And still know your opening cold — the tech can fail; you shouldn't.

    The honest limit: AI can generate your speaker notes, and Speaker Coach can count your fillers — but neither can rehearse for you, read the room, or answer the hard question from the back. The deck is a tool. You own the room. The only way past nerves is reps out loud, and no software does those for you.
