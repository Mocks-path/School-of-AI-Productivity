Week 4 · Lesson 04 · AI Slides

The deck is drafted. Now earn the room.

AI can generate a plausible presentation in minutes — but plausible is not presentation-ready. This final lesson is about the last mile: the polish pass that cuts noise, the speaker notes that steady your nerves, the accessibility work that lets everyone follow along, and the honest truth that AI can rehearse nothing for you. You still have to stand up and own it. Here's how to walk in prepared.

01 / 04

From rough draft to presentation-ready

A first AI draft is a starting point, not a finish line. The gap between "the AI made slides" and "I can present these confidently" is a short, repeatable polish pass. It has five moves, always in the same order — because each one depends on the last. Fixing flow before you cut words is wasted effort; rehearsing before you verify facts just memorizes mistakes.

The order matters. First cut words, because a slide with half the text is easier to reason about. Then fix flow, so each slide leads naturally to the next. Only then verify facts — AI confidently invents statistics, and a wrong number on a slide is a wrong number you'll say out loud. Next, generate speaker notes so you're never reading the slide to the audience. Finally, rehearse — the one step no tool can do for you. Run the pipeline to watch it assemble.

Why the order is non-negotiable

Beginners are tempted to jump straight to rehearsing a fresh draft — it feels like progress. But rehearsing a bloated, unverified deck bakes in every flaw: you memorize the wordy slide and the wrong statistic. Polish first, rehearse last. By the time you're saying it out loud, everything on screen should already be true, tight, and in the right order. The pipeline protects you from practicing your own mistakes.

02 / 04

The edit pass, shown live

"Cut the words" sounds obvious until you see how much a raw AI slide actually carries. AI tends to over-explain — full sentences, hedged clauses, three bullets that say one thing. The edit pass strips that back to what an audience can absorb in the three seconds their eyes are on the screen. Flip between the before and after below.

The rule of thumb: a slide is a headline plus evidence, not a document. If your audience can read it faster than you can say it, they'll read ahead and stop listening. Cut anything you'll say out loud anyway — that belongs in your speaker notes, not on the slide. Watch the same slide before and after a real edit pass.

  • Customer churn decreased somewhat, dropping from around 5.2% down to roughly 4.1% over the same period of time
  • We successfully onboarded a total of 47 new enterprise customers, which exceeded our internal target of 40 customers
  • Overall team morale and engagement scores also improved based on our latest internal survey feedback results
Slide 4 · After18 words
Q3: Growth on every metric that matters
  • Revenue +12% quarter over quarter
  • Churn down 5.2% → 4.1%
  • 47 new enterprise customers (target: 40)
  • Team engagement up
What the edit pass did
  • Cut the title from a hedged sentence to a claim you can defend
  • Removed "approximately," "roughly," "we believe" — the hedging goes in your voice, not the slide
  • Turned prose into numbers: +12%, 5.2% → 4.1%, 47
  • Moved the survey context to speaker notes — it's a talking point, not a bullet

What you keep, what you say

Notice nothing true was lost — it just moved. The "exceeded our target of 40" detail survives as a parenthetical; the morale context moves into your spoken notes. That's the whole discipline: the slide holds the claim and the proof, and you hold the explanation. A packed slide makes you redundant. A lean slide makes you necessary — which is exactly where you want to be when you're standing in front of the room.

03 / 04

Speaker notes, and a coach for your delivery

Speaker notes are the quiet superpower of a calm presenter. They live under each slide, invisible to the audience, holding the sentences you actually plan to say. AI writes a solid first draft in seconds — you edit it into your own voice. Pick a slide below and watch notes appear.

Good notes aren't a transcript to read word-for-word; that sounds robotic. They're a scaffold: the opening line, the one statistic you must get right, the transition to the next slide. Enough that if your mind goes blank, a glance gets you back on track. Choose a slide and see what AI drafts.

Notes get you through the words. But how you say them — your pace, your filler words, your energy — is a separate skill. Some AI tools now listen to a practice run and give you feedback. Treat it the way you'd treat a supportive friend in the back row: it's guidance to help you improve, never a scorecard that decides whether you're good enough. Guidance, not a grade

  
Read that panel again and notice the framing: "nice," "try," "a little more." No score, no pass/fail, no comparison to anyone else. That's the only healthy way to use a speaker coach. The metrics point at habits you can change — a pause instead of an "um," a beat of emphasis on a number — not at your worth as a presenter. If a tool ever makes you feel measured rather than helped, close it. Nervousness is normal; the coach exists to channel it, not to shame it.






  
04 / 04

  
Make it work for everyone, then walk in ready

  
A polished deck that half the room can't follow isn't polished. An accessibility pass is quick, it's the right thing to do, and it makes your deck better for everyone — clearer contrast, sensible order, and text alternatives help the tired and the distracted as much as anyone. AI can run a first check; you make the calls. Click through the four checks below.


  
The four workhorses: alt text so screen readers can describe your images, contrast so text is readable on a projector in a bright room, reading order so assistive tech moves through the slide the way you intend, and captions for any audio or video. Each has an AI-assisted check — and a human judgment call it can't make for you.


  

    

      Figure 5 · The accessibility pass, checked
    

    

      
      
      
      
    


    

      
Alt textDescribes images to screen readers

      

        
Every image needs a description

        
Alt text is the sentence a screen reader speaks in place of an image. AI can draft a literal description of what's in the picture — but only you know why the image is there. "Bar chart" is useless; "Bar chart showing revenue up 12% in Q3" carries the point.

        
✓
3 of 4 images described
AI generated draft alt text for the logo, headshot, and chart.

        
!
1 decorative image flagged
The background texture should be marked decorative so it's skipped, not described.

      

    


    

      
ContrastReadable text on any screen

      

        
Faint text is invisible text

        
Low-contrast text — pale grey on white, thin colour on colour — vanishes on a projector or for anyone with low vision. A contrast checker measures the ratio against a standard. AI flags failures instantly; you decide whether to darken the text or rethink the palette.

        
✓
Body text passes AA
Dark ink on cream clears the 4.5:1 ratio comfortably.

        
!
Yellow caption fails
The pale yellow subtitle on white sits at 1.9:1 — darken it or add a backing.

      

    


    

      
Reading orderAssistive tech follows your intent

      

        
Order the eye can't see, but the ear needs

        
Screen readers walk a slide in a set sequence — often not the visual order you designed. If a text box was added last, it may be read last, out of context. The checker shows the read order; you drag it back into a sequence that makes sense spoken aloud.

        
✓
Title reads first
Heading, then bullets top-to-bottom — the natural order.

        
!
Callout reads out of place
The corner callout is announced before the main point. Move it later in the order.

      

    


    

      
CaptionsNobody misses the audio

      

        
If it makes sound, caption it

        
Embedded video or audio needs captions for anyone who can't hear it — and for the room where the speakers are broken. AI transcribes reliably now, but timing and names still slip. Generate the captions automatically, then read them once against the video.

        
✓
Auto-captions generated
AI transcribed the 40-second product clip in one pass.

        
!
Two names to verify
Proper nouns are AI's weak spot — confirm the two flagged names before you present.

The honest limit: AI can't rehearse for you

  
Every tool in this course can draft, cut, check, and coach — but not one can stand up and present for you. AI can hand you a tight deck, clean notes, and feedback on your last run, and that's genuine leverage. It cannot give you the reps, steady your voice on the day, or read the room and slow down when eyes glaze over. Those are yours. The deck is a prop; you are the presentation. Do the polish so the tools carry the drudgery — then do the rehearsing, because that part only works when it's you.






  
What to carry forward

  
A first AI draft is raw material, not a finished talk. Run the polish pipeline in order — cut, flow, verify, notes, rehearse — because each step depends on the last. Let AI thin your slides, draft your notes, coach your delivery, and check your accessibility. But hold the line on the things only you can do: verify every fact, do the reps out loud, and own the room. Walk in polished, and walk in prepared.
