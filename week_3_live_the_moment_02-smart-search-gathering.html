Smart Search & Information Gathering — ask sharp, read the sources, trust nothing blindly

Four hands-on activities. Match research needs to the right tool, rebuild a vague query into a strong research prompt block by block, run a citation clinic on three real-looking sources, and drill the verify-first habits that keep AI answer engines from quietly leading you wrong.

1

Match the research need to the right tool

A plain chatbot, an answer engine, and a deep-research agent are not interchangeable. Picking the wrong one is why people get stale facts or invented citations. Study the three tool families below, then drag each research need into the tool that fits it best. Guess freely — wrong answers explain themselves on hover.

C

Plain LLM chat

"Reason over what the model already knows." No live web — answers come from training data with a cutoff date.

  • Great for drafting, brainstorming, explaining
  • Fast, no links to chase
  • Can be stale or confidently wrong — no sources
A

Answer engine (search)

"Search the live web, then summarize with citations." Perplexity, ChatGPT search, Google AI mode.

  • Current facts, prices, news, releases
  • Every claim links to a source you can open
  • Only as good as the pages it happened to find
D

Deep research agent

"Run many searches over minutes, read dozens of pages, write a cited report." A bigger, slower job.

  • Comparisons, landscapes, literature-style scans
  • Worth the wait for multi-angle questions
  • Overkill for a single quick fact

Turn a vague query into a strong research prompt

"Best CRM software?" gets you a generic listicle. A research prompt that actually helps names four things: TOPIC → CONTEXT → CONSTRAINTS → SOURCES. The eight blocks of a strong research prompt are scrambled below — click them in the order they should appear. A wrong click tells you why it doesn't fit yet.

Follow citations back to the primary source (the original study, filing, or vendor page — not a blog quoting a blog). Capture the exact quote + link. Verify the claim in a second, independent source before you rely on it.

The verify-first quiz — read the citations, not just the summary

Answer engines are fast and often right — but they summarize whatever pages they happened to surface, and they can state a wrong fact with total confidence. This quiz drills the habit that keeps you safe: always open the source and confirm the claim. Five situations, one honest answer each — the misconceptions are where the real learning is.

Quick reflect — make it stick

A sentence each is enough. Writing it in your own words is what moves it into memory. (Nothing is saved or sent.)

You need to recommend a project-management tool for a 12-person remote team on a tight budget, by Friday. Write the research prompt you'd give an answer engine — include topic, context, constraints, and an explicit ask for sources.
An answer engine gives you a confident number with a footnote. Describe the exact steps you'd take before repeating that number in a report your boss will read.
