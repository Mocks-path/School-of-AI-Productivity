Week 2 · Lesson 04 · AI Office

The meeting ends. The notes write themselves.

Most of what happens in a meeting evaporates the moment it's over — who agreed to what, by when, and what you'd have caught if you'd stayed awake through minute forty. AI recap turns the recording into a clean recap, a decision log, and a list of action items with owners and dates. Done well, it hands your afternoon back. Done carelessly, it invents a deadline nobody agreed to — so we'll also learn where to stay honest, and when recording is even allowed.

01 / 05

From talk to to-do: the recap pipeline

A meeting assistant isn't one feature — it's a small assembly line that runs the instant you hit "leave." Raw audio goes in one end; a structured, skimmable summary comes out the other. Understanding the stages tells you what to trust and what to double-check.

First the tool records and transcribes — turning speech into timestamped text, ideally labelling who said what. Then it recaps — compressing forty minutes of rambling into a few paragraphs of what actually mattered. Next it separates decisions from action items — a decision is something the group settled; an action item is a task somebody now owns. Finally it sets up follow-up — a shareable note, an email draft, tasks pushed to your list. Watch the line run.

Why the stages matter for trust

Each stage can fail differently, so knowing them tells you where to look. Transcription mangles names and numbers. Recap can flatten a heated debate into "the team discussed options." Action extraction is where the real risk lives: it will happily assign a task to the wrong person or invent a deadline that was only floated as a maybe. The recap is a draft written by a fast, tireless, slightly overconfident assistant — brilliant for the first 90%, and exactly why you read the last 10%.

A meeting is a system with three parts

The best AI-meeting habit isn't a clever prompt — it's treating every meeting as an operating system with a before, a during, and an after. AI helps at all three, but only if you set the stage before anyone starts talking.

Beginners think the tool starts working when the meeting starts. In truth, the biggest wins are booked before — a clear agenda gives the AI structure to summarise against — and cashed after, when the recap turns into action. The meeting itself is just the raw material in the middle.

The unglamorous truth

An AI that captures a rambling, agenda-less meeting just gives you a tidy summary of chaos. The tool amplifies whatever structure you bring. Say owners and dates aloud, restate decisions before you move on, and the "during" stage practically writes the "after" for you. Meetings that summarise well are meetings that were run well.

"What did I miss?" — and closing the loop

Two everyday superpowers fall out of a good recap. The first: you skipped the meeting (or zoned out) and need the gist in thirty seconds. The second: you ran the meeting and now have to tell everyone what happens next. AI does both from the same transcript.

The catch-up card answers the questions you'd otherwise DM three colleagues to learn: what got decided, what's on me, and did anything change. And instead of you writing the follow-up email from scratch, the recap becomes a first draft you edit in a minute rather than compose in fifteen.

Same source, two audiences

Notice both outputs came from one transcript, aimed at different readers. The catch-up card is for you — terse, personal, "what's on me." The follow-up email is for the room and the absentees — decisions, owners, dates, laid out so nobody has to attend the meeting again to know what happened. Learning to ask the AI for the right shape — "give me my personal to-dos" versus "write the team recap" — is most of the skill.

Four tools — and the rules of recording

The market is crowded, but four names cover almost everyone: Microsoft Teams intelligent recap, OneNote with Copilot, Google Meet, and dedicated bots like Otter or Fireflies. They overlap a lot; the right pick is mostly about which ecosystem you already live in. Click a tab.

The short version: Teams intelligent recap is the default if your org runs on Microsoft — recap, action items, and speaker timelines built into the call. OneNote + Copilot is where those notes go to live, get organised, and get queried later. Google Meet gives you "take notes for me" and transcripts inside Workspace. Otter / Fireflies are ecosystem-agnostic bots that join any call and often go deepest on transcription. Pick by where your calls and notes already happen.

Reach for it when

The notebook that answers questions

OneNote is less about capturing the live call and more about what happens to the notes afterward. Drop a recap in, and Copilot can summarise the page, pull to-do lists out of your scribbles, rewrite messy notes into clean prose, and answer "what did we decide about X" across a whole notebook of past meetings.

Reach for it when

You want a durable, searchable home for meeting notes — and to ask questions across weeks of them, not just one call.

Strong points

  • Pulls action lists out of freeform text
  • Query across many past meetings
Trade-offs
  • Doesn't record the live call itself
  • Copilot features need a licence
  • Only as good as the notes you feed it

The Workspace-native option

If your world is Gmail, Docs, and Calendar, Google Meet's "take notes for me" captures the call and drops a summary and transcript straight into a Google Doc, saved to Drive and linked in the calendar event. Same idea as Teams recap, just inside the Google ecosystem instead of Microsoft's.

Reach for it when

Your team runs on Google Workspace — the notes land in a Doc next to everything else you already work in.

Strong points
  • Native to Meet — no bot to add
  • Summary & transcript saved to a Google Doc
  • Auto-linked to the calendar event
Trade-offs
  • Needs a qualifying Workspace plan
  • Only for meetings held in Meet
  • Feature availability varies by edition
Otter / FirefliesEcosystem-agnostic bots

The bot that joins any call

These are dedicated assistants that dial into Teams, Meet, or Zoom as a participant, record, and often lead the pack on transcript quality, live keyword search, and CRM integrations. The trade-off: a visible bot joins your meeting, and your audio flows through a third party — which raises real consent and data questions.

Reach for it when

You bounce between platforms, want the deepest transcription and search, or need meeting data flowing into other tools like a CRM.

Strong points
  • Works across Teams, Meet, Zoom
  • Often the strongest transcription & search
  • Integrations into CRMs and task tools
Trade-offs
  • A visible bot joins the call
  • Audio routed through a third-party service
  • Data-handling & consent need real scrutiny

Recording is a legal act, not just a button

Here's the part the product pages skip: pressing "record" can be regulated. Some places allow it if just one party (you) consents; others require all parties to agree — and your employer almost certainly has its own policy on top of the law. The safe default is boring and bulletproof: announce it, get a clear yes, and know your organisation's rules before the bot ever joins. Walk the checklist.

Figure 6 · The consent & etiquette checklist
✓

Announce that you're recording — out loud and up front

Say it at the top of the call and, ideally, note it in the invite. Silent recording is where etiquette and law both go wrong.

✓

Know one-party vs all-party consent for everyone's location

Some regions let one participant consent; others require everyone. On mixed or cross-border calls, default to all-party consent.

✓

Check your organisation's recording policy first

Company rules sit on top of the law. Some data, clients, or channels may be off-limits to recording bots entirely.

✓

Give people a way to opt out

Someone should be able to ask you to pause the recording or leave it off — sensitive topics and HR matters especially.

✓

Mind where the transcript is stored and who can see it

A recap is a record. Know where it lives, who has access, and how long it's kept — especially with third-party bots.

✓

Verify owners & dates before you send anything on

AI mislabels speakers and invents deadlines. A thirty-second check turns a plausible draft into a trustworthy record.

Honest accuracy caution

Treat every recap as a confident draft, not a court transcript. Transcription garbles names, numbers, and accents; speaker labels get swapped; and action-item extraction is where wrong owners and imaginary deadlines creep in. The failure mode is subtle — the recap reads clean and authoritative even when it's wrong. Skim it against your own memory of the meeting, fix the owners and dates, and only then let it become the shared record everyone acts on.

What to carry forward

AI meeting tools are an assembly line: record, recap, extract actions, follow up. They hand back the hour you'd have spent typing notes — but only if you run the meeting well and read the last 10% they get wrong. Bring an agenda, say owners and dates aloud, pick the tool that matches your ecosystem, and never let a recap go out the door before you've checked who owns what by when — or before you've made sure recording it was allowed in the first place.

  • Pipeline: record & transcribe → recap → decisions + action items → follow-up
  • Meetings are a system: the wins are set up before and cashed after

  • One transcript, two outputs: personal catch-up card vs shared follow-up email
