AI in Word — describe the document, let AI draft it

Four hands-on activities. Walk the draft loop, assemble a document prompt from its four parts, play editor on an AI draft riddled with invented facts, and drill the one discipline that keeps your name off a bad memo — because a draft that reads well is not the same as a draft that's true.

Plain English in, a draft out — and the loop that follows

In Word, AI (Copilot, or any assistant you paste into) turns a sentence of intent into a full first draft. But the magic isn't the first draft — it's the loop you run on it. You Ask, you Read critically, you Refine with a follow-up, and you Verify every fact before it leaves your hands.

Step 1
Ask

Describe the document in plain English — who it's for and what it must do.

→
Step 2
Read

Read the draft like an editor, not a fan. Where is it vague, generic, or possibly made up?

→
Step 3
Refine

Send a follow-up: shorter, warmer, add a section, cut the fluff. It rewrites in place.

→
Step 4
Verify

Check every name, number, date, and citation against a real source before you send.

There are a handful of jobs AI does well in a document. Study the three families, then drag each real task into the pattern that fits it. Guess freely — wrong answers explain themselves on hover.

C

Create from scratch

"I have nothing yet — give me a first draft I can shape." Beats the blank page.

  • Draft an email, memo, or announcement
  • Generate an outline before you write
  • Build a reusable template or table
T

Transform what exists

"I have text — change its shape, tone, or length." Editing at the speed of thought.

  • Rewrite in a warmer / more formal tone
  • Shorten to half, or expand a bullet
  • Fix grammar, flow, and clarity
D

Digest a long doc

"There's too much to read — pull out what matters." Turns 20 pages into 5 bullets.

  • Summarize a report or contract
  • Extract action items or key dates
  • Reference your own files for context

Build the document prompt — one part at a time

"Write me an email about the delay" gets you a generic, could-be-anyone paragraph. A prompt that yields the right document names four things: AUDIENCE → GOAL → TONE & LENGTH → OUTPUT & SOURCE. Once you have a draft, transforming it is a single follow-up sentence. These are the moves you'll reach for most:

Re-tone
Change the voice

"Make this warmer and less corporate" or "rewrite in a formal, executive tone." Same facts, new register.

Re-length
Shorten or expand

"Cut this to 120 words" or "expand each bullet into a short paragraph." Say the target, not just "shorter."

Restructure
Change the shape

"Turn these paragraphs into a bulleted summary with a heading" or "make a 3-column table from this."

Reference
Point at your files

"Using the attached report, draft a one-page summary." Grounds the draft in your real content, not guesses.

Spot the invented fact — three drafts, three traps

AI writes fluently and confidently, which is exactly the danger: a hallucination reads as smoothly as a fact. Each draft below came back polished and professional — and each contains one thing you must never trust on sight. Read like the person whose name goes on it. Pick what to flag. Every choice explains itself, so wrong guesses teach too.

Draft A · Client email

The confident statistic

"As you know, 73% of enterprises in your sector adopted this exact solution in 2024, so the market has clearly moved. We'd be glad to walk you through the rollout."
What must you verify before sending?
Draft B · Research brief

The citation that looks real

"This aligns with findings from Henderson & Vale (2021), Journal of Applied Operations, pp. 44–61, who reported a 2.3× efficiency gain."
What must you verify before sending?
Draft C · Company announcement

The plausible-but-invented detail

The verify-before-you-send quiz — your name, your risk

AI writes drafts that read well. Reading well is not the same as being true, on-message, or yours. This quiz drills the discipline that keeps a good tool from becoming a liability: read as an editor, verify every claim, and make the voice your own before you hit send. Five situations, one honest answer each — the misconceptions are where the real learning is.
