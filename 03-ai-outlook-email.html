Week 2 · Lesson 03 · AI Office

Your inbox is a queue. AI is the triage nurse.

A full inbox isn't a to-do list — it's a pile of decisions waiting to be made. In this lesson you turn that pile into a repeatable flow: let AI summarize the thread, you decide what it deserves, AI drafts the reply in the right tone, you review for wrong recipients and invented commitments, and only then does it send. Same discipline for the calendar — meetings, tasks, and prep briefs, all one prompt away.

01 / 05

Triage is a pipeline, not a scroll

Beginners open the inbox and scroll, re-reading the same threads three times before acting on any of them. The pros run every message through the same five steps — and Copilot in Outlook can help at each one. Master the loop and "inbox zero" stops being a fantasy.

The mistake is treating AI as an autopilot that reads and replies for you. It isn't. It's an assistant that does the reading and the drafting so you can do the deciding. The loop is fixed: first AI summarizes the thread so you grasp it in one glance, then you decide what it needs — reply, delegate, schedule, or ignore — then AI drafts the reply, then you review it for tone, recipients, and invented facts, and only then do you send. Tip — AI drafts, you decide and review. Never let a message send itself.

Why two steps stay human

AI can read faster than you and write more politely than you, but it can't be accountable. Deciding whether a thread deserves a reply, a delegation, or the archive is a judgement call about your priorities. And reviewing the draft — did it promise something you can't deliver, is it going to the right person — is the one gate that keeps a confident, well-written mistake from reaching a client. Skip either, and AI just helps you make errors faster.

02 / 05

Draft the right reply, not just a reply

A good reply isn't only correct — it's the right tone for the relationship and the right length for the situation. "Draft a reply" gets you generic mush. "Draft a firm but polite reply declining the deadline, short" gets you something you can almost send as-is. You steer; AI types.

The same underlying message reads completely differently depending on two dials. Tone — formal for a client you've never met, friendly for a teammate, firm when you need to hold a line without being rude. Length — short when a yes/no will do, detailed when you owe context and next steps. Set both below and watch the same reply re-cast itself. The reply here declines a request to move a launch date.

Always give the AI the constraint, not just the topic

The difference between a useless draft and a great one is the constraint you hand over. Don't say "reply to this." Say "reply declining, keep our relationship warm, mention the QA date as the reason, and offer the 22nd as an alternative." The AI can write in any voice — your job is to tell it which voice, and what non-negotiable facts must appear. Then read it once as if you were the recipient.

03 / 05

Collapse the 20-message thread to what matters

The worst thing about a long thread isn't its length — it's that the decisions are buried inside pleasantries, reversals, and "per my last email." You don't need the whole conversation. You need three things: what was decided, who owns what, and what's still open.

A twenty-message thread almost always distils to a handful of lines. AI is genuinely excellent at this — "summarize this thread into decisions, owners, and open questions" turns forty minutes of scrolling into a ten-second read. The one rule: the summary is a map, not the territory. If a decision affects money or a deadline, click into the original message to confirm the AI didn't smooth over a caveat. Ask for the shape you need

"Summarize this" gives you a paragraph you still have to parse. Ask for a shape instead: "list the decisions as bullets, then a table of action items with owners and due dates, then open questions." The more structure you request, the more useful — and the more obviously verifiable — the summary becomes. A summary you can scan in ten seconds is worth re-checking; a wall of prose isn't.

04 / 05

An email is often a task or a meeting in disguise

Half the messages in your inbox aren't really asking for a reply — they're asking for an action. "Can we sync on the budget this week?" is a meeting. "Please send the revised deck by Friday" is a task with a due date. AI in Outlook can turn either into the right artifact in one click.

Instead of letting a message rot in the inbox as a reminder-that-isn't, convert it. Turn it into a task in To Do with the deadline captured. Turn it into a meeting — Copilot reads everyone's free/busy and proposes times so you skip the "does Tuesday work?" ping-pong. Get a prep brief before a meeting — a card of what it's about, who's coming, and what you last agreed. Pick an action below to see what AI produces.

05 / 05

Never send an AI draft you haven't reviewed

An AI draft is confident, fluent, and occasionally makes promises you never intended — or is quietly addressed to the wrong Sarah. The output looks ready to send either way. The final, non-negotiable step is the review: catch the hallucinated commitment and the wrong recipient before you hit send, not after.

There's a real privacy dimension here too. Copilot in Outlook runs on your company's Microsoft 365 tenant under its data protections — your email stays inside your organisation's boundary. Pasting a work email into public ChatGPT or Claude is powerful, but you're sending company content to an outside service — check your employer's policy first, and strip names and confidential details. The no-Copilot-license alternative for personal use is Gmail with Gemini, which does much the same on a consumer account. Whichever tool, every draft is a draft until you've reviewed it. The three-second pre-send check

You don't need to re-read the whole thread — you need three glances. Recipients: is every name the one you meant, and is anyone on Cc who shouldn't see this? Commitments: did the draft promise a date, a deliverable, or a number you didn't authorise? Attachments: if the email says "attached," is the file actually there? If any glance fails, the AI over-helped. Trust the fluent draft least — with AI, review first, send second.

What to carry forward

You now know that a managed inbox is manufactured, not willpowered. AI reads, drafts, summarizes, and schedules — but you decide and you review, because you're the one whose name is on the "From" line. The tool accelerates every step, and quietly introduces a wrong recipient or an invented promise at every step too, which is why the human owns the send button.

  • Run every message through the loop: summarize → decide → draft → review → send
  • Decide and review stay human — AI can't be accountable for what leaves your outbox
  • Great drafts come from giving the AI the constraint: tone, length, and the fixed facts
  • Ask a long thread for a shape — decisions, owners, open questions — not a paragraph
  • An email is often a task or a meeting; let Copilot convert it and propose times
  • Copilot keeps mail in your tenant; pasting into public AI sends it out — Gmail+Gemini is the personal alternative
  • Before send, glance three times: right recipients, no invented commitments, attachment present
