Week 5 · Lesson 03 · AI Project Management

From scattered signals to a status people trust.

A status update is not a chore you do on Friday afternoon — it's how a project stays honest. In this lesson you turn scattered signals into a clear, truthful update through a repeatable pipeline: collect the signals, summarize them, flag the risks, tailor the message to who's reading it, and — the step everyone skips — review before you send. AI drafts each step; you own the truth.

01 / 05

A status update is a pipeline, not a Friday scramble

Beginners treat status as a memory dump: "here's everything that happened this week." A good project manager runs the same five steps every time — gather the signals, summarize, flag what's at risk, tune it for the reader, and review before hitting send. AI can draft each step; the discipline is what makes it trustworthy.

The mistake is jumping straight from a pile of Slack threads and Jira tickets to a message you fire off. Skip the middle and you get an update that's long, vague, and quietly hides the one thing leadership needed to know. The pipeline forces order: first you collect the raw signals, then you summarize them into a few plain sentences, then you flag risks so blockers surface early, then you tailor the message to your audience, and finally you review before sending. Tip — the order is not optional. An update that skips "flag risks" reads fine on Friday and blows up on Monday.

Why order matters

Each stage protects the next. Collecting protects the summary; the summary protects the risk flags; the risk flags protect the tailoring; and review protects everyone downstream. Break the chain anywhere and the update still looks finished — which is exactly what makes it dangerous. A polished status with the real blocker buried is worse than no update at all, because it buys false confidence.

02 / 05

Same facts, different reader — tune the update

The single hardest skill in status communication isn't writing — it's audience. A VP and a developer need the same truth in completely different shapes. The VP wants the headline, the risk, and the ask. The developer wants the specifics, the dependency, and who's unblocked. AI can spin one set of facts into both — if you tell it who's reading.

Give the AI the raw facts once, then ask for two versions: an executive update (outcome, RAG status, one risk, one decision needed — three sentences) and a team update (ticket-level detail, owners, blockers, next steps). The audience test

Before sending any update, ask one question: what does this reader do with it? A VP decides and reallocates — so lead with the risk and the ask. An engineer coordinates and unblocks — so lead with the dependency and the owner. If you send the developer's ticket dump to the VP, they'll miss the risk in the noise. If you send the VP's three-line summary to the team, nobody knows what to actually do. AI drafts both in seconds; you pick which reality each person needs.

03 / 05

The RAG board catches trouble before it's a crisis

A red/amber/green status board is the fastest honest signal a project has. Green means on track, amber means watch this, red means it needs a decision now. The value isn't the colours — it's that amber and red force a risk into the open while there's still time to act. AI can draft the board from your signals; you set the honest colour.

Here's the whole idea: each workstream gets a RAG colour and, when it's amber or red, a line in the risk register — what could go wrong, and what you're doing about it. Green with no register entry means genuinely fine. Amber is the most valuable state: an early warning you can still act on. Amber is the goal, not red

The projects that fail quietly are the ones that jump straight from green to red — because nobody flagged the amber in between. A blocker caught while it's amber ("staging env flaky, could slip the demo") is a five-minute Slack message. The same blocker caught at red ("staging is down, demo is tomorrow") is a crisis meeting. AI is good at spotting slippage early from your signals — but only the team can confirm whether "at risk" is really amber or already red.

04 / 05

Meeting notes become tasks with an owner and a date

The commitments made in a meeting are worthless if they live in your notes. A task without an owner is a wish; a task without a date is a someday. The most useful thing AI does in project management is read raw meeting notes and pull out the action items — each with a name attached and a deadline — so nothing quietly evaporates.

Feed the AI your messy notes and ask for a table: task, owner, due date. It's fast and mostly right — but it will invent an owner when the notes are ambiguous, or drop a date it never saw. The gap the AI leaves

Watch for three failure modes. The AI assigns an owner the notes never named ("someone should look at the API" becomes "Priya: fix API"). It invents a date to fill the column when none was said. And it merges two half-decisions into one confident task. Every extracted task is a draft commitment — before it goes on the board, the named owner has to see it and agree. AI drafts the list; the standup confirms it.

05 / 05

Spot the watermelon: green outside, red inside

The most dangerous status update is the one that sounds fine. AI is trained to be helpful and reassuring — which means it will happily smooth a shaky project into a confident green, read "code merged" as "done," and summarize around the real blocker instead of surfacing it. A watermelon status is green on the surface and red in the middle. Your last job is to cut it open.

Before any AI-drafted update goes out, run it through a suspicion pass: does "done" actually mean shipped and verified, or just "merged"? Is the reassuring tone hiding a slip? Did the summary bury the one dependency that could sink the timeline? The three-question honesty check

You don't need to re-run the project — you need three questions the AI won't ask itself. Does "done" mean verified? Merged, deployed, and tested are three different things. Is the reassurance earned? "On track" needs a date and a burndown, not a vibe. Where's the risk it's not mentioning? A summary with no risk in it is either a genuinely healthy project or a watermelon — and you find out which by asking the team, not the model. Verify before you send: the AI drafts, the humans confirm, you own what goes out.

What to carry forward

You now know that a trustworthy status is manufactured, not typed on a Friday. Scattered signals become an update people can act on only when they're collected, summarized, risk-flagged, tailored to the reader, and reviewed before sending. AI accelerates every step — and quietly over-reassures at every step too, which is why the human owns the send button.

  • Run every update through the pipeline: collect → summarize → flag risks → tailor → review & send
  • Same facts, different reader: exec wants headline + risk + ask; team wants detail + owner + blocker
  • RAG boards make risk visible early — amber is the goal, a caught blocker beats a crisis
  • Turn meeting notes into tasks with an owner and a date; confirm each owner actually agreed
  • Watch for watermelon status: green outside, red inside — AI over-reassures and misreads "done"
  • AI drafts the status; the team verifies the truth and you review before you send
