Synthesis & Analysis — turn ten sources into one honest answer

Gathering sources is the easy half of research. The hard half is weaving them together without flattening what makes each one distinct. Four hands-on drills: run the six-step synthesis workflow, decide what a good summary keeps and drops, build a source-by-theme matrix, catch AI when it smooths real disagreement into a fake consensus — then ground your answers in your own documents with NotebookLM.

The synthesis workflow — six steps, in order

Synthesis is not "summarise everything and mash it together." It is the disciplined move from a pile of sources to a claim you can defend — with each part of the claim traceable to something real. AI can accelerate every step, but "summarise these ten articles" tends to produce a bland average that agrees with everyone and commits to nothing. Follow the order below, and you keep the tension, the caveats and the citations that make research trustworthy.

STEP 1
Collect
Gather your sources and note what each one actually is — a study, an opinion, a vendor page. Provenance matters later.
→
STEP 2
Ground
Feed the real documents to a grounded tool (like NotebookLM) so answers come from your sources, not the model's memory.
→
STEP 3
Extract
Pull the key claim, evidence and caveats from each source. Keep the load-bearing detail; drop the filler.
→
STEP 4
Compare
Line sources up against shared themes. Where do they agree? Where do they genuinely disagree, and why?
→
STEP 5
Synthesize
Write the claim that the evidence supports — including the disagreements and the limits, not just the tidy part.
→
STEP 6
Verify
Trace every claim back to a source and check the citation actually says it. AI can attribute things nobody wrote.

Summarise well — pick the version that keeps what matters

A good summary is not just shorter — it is faithful. It keeps the load-bearing parts (the actual finding, the size of the effect, the conditions and caveats) and drops the filler (throat-clearing, restated background, hedging that adds nothing). A bad summary often keeps the confident-sounding conclusion while quietly deleting the "in a small sample" or "only in one country" that changes what the conclusion means. Below are three pairs. Build the synthesis matrix — place each finding under its theme

A synthesis matrix is a grid: sources down one side, recurring themes across the top, and in each cell what that source says about that theme. It turns a stack of articles into a map of the conversation — you can see at a glance who agrees, who disagrees, and where a theme has only one voice. Real consensus or AI over-smoothing? Spot the false consensus

When you ask AI to synthesise conflicting sources, its instinct is to be agreeable — it blends disagreement into a smooth "experts generally find..." that no single source actually supports. That is false consensus, and it is one of synthesis's most dangerous failure modes: it reads as balanced while erasing the real debate. Ground it and check it — synthesis you can defend

A general chatbot answers from everything it ever read; it can't tell you which of your ten documents a claim came from, and it may blend in facts you never uploaded. A grounded tool like NotebookLM answers only from the sources you give it and cites the passage — so you can click through and verify. But grounding is not magic: it can still miss caveats, over-smooth, or cite a passage that doesn't quite say what it claims. Answer these four. Each locks in with the reasoning.

✓

The 60-second synthesis check before you cite it

Before any AI-synthesised paragraph goes into your report, run it past these. Synthesis errors are quiet — they read fluently and confidently — so you have to look for what's missing, not just what's there.

  • ≠
    Did it keep the disagreement? If your sources argued and the summary says everyone agrees, the tension was smoothed away. Real synthesis names who differs and why — a suspiciously tidy consensus is a red flag.
  • *
    Are the caveats still attached? "In a small pilot," "only for one team," "self-reported" — these change what a finding means. AI loves to drop them. Check that each claim still carries its conditions.
  • →
    Does every claim trace to a source? For each sentence, ask "which document said this?" If you can't point to one, the AI may have invented it or blended in outside knowledge. Grounded tools cite; verify the citation.
  • ?
    Does the citation actually say it? Click through. AI can attach a real-looking source to a claim that source never made. A citation you didn't read is a citation you can't trust.
