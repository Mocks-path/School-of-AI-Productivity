Week 5 · Lesson 02 · AI Project Management

State the goal. Get the plan.

A blank project plan is intimidating: scope, tasks, estimates, dependencies, a timeline. AI can draft all of it from a sentence about your goal. This lesson teaches the part that still belongs to you — feeding it the constraints, catching the tasks it invents, and refusing its cheerful, always-too-short estimates. The AI plans fast. You keep it honest.

01 / 06

A goal in, a project plan out

The old skill was staring at a blank Gantt chart and building it cell by cell. The new skill is describing a goal precisely enough — with its real constraints — that AI can draft the whole work breakdown for you to edit.

Here is the shift. You used to open a template and type every task, estimate, and dependency by hand. Now you write a sentence — "plan a company website relaunch" — and AI returns a structured work breakdown with tasks and a rough schedule. But the sentence has to carry the load: what "done" means, what constrains you, who's on the team, and when it's due. Watch a vague brief and a loaded one produce very different plans below.

02 / 06

The anatomy of a good planning brief

A useful planning request answers four questions before AI drafts a single task: what does "done" mean, what constrains you, who is on the team, and when is it due.

Think of it as briefing a contractor. "Build me something nice" gets you a generic quote for a house you didn't want; "a two-bathroom extension, this budget, finished before winter, on this plot" gets you a real plan. A planning prompt works the same way. Skip these and AI guesses — usually plausibly, but always in a way that invents scope and underestimates time, and always in a way you'll have to correct.

Part 1

Definition of done

What outcome counts as finished, in concrete terms: "the new site is live and the old one is redirected," not "launch the website." Say what "done" looks like or AI will scope its own version.

Part 2

Constraints

The real limits: budget, a frozen tech stack, a compliance sign-off, a code freeze in December. AI can't see your world — the constraints are what shape a plan into a possible one.

Part 3

Team & capacity

Who is actually available and for how much of their week. "Two developers at half-time and one designer" produces a very different timeline than the full-time team AI assumes by default.

Part 4

Deadline & milestones

The hard date and any checkpoints along the way. Give AI the deadline and it can work backwards; withhold it and it plans forward into an optimistic timeline that ignores your calendar.

Notice that none of these require you to know a planning methodology or a scheduling tool. You describe the outcome and the real-world limits; AI supplies the structure. That division of labour — you own the constraints and the intent, AI owns the drafting and the recall — is the method of this lesson, and it repeats through every figure below.

03 / 06

The planning pipeline, one stage at a time

A project plan isn't one output — it's a sequence. Scope narrows to a work breakdown, which becomes tasks, which get estimated, wired with dependencies, and laid onto a timeline. AI can draft each stage; you own the judgement at each handoff.

The mistake beginners make is asking AI for "a full project plan" in one shot and accepting the wall of text it returns. Better is to walk the pipeline stage by stage, checking the output of each before feeding it into the next — because an error in scope quietly poisons everything downstream. Hover any stage below to see who really owns it.

AI is strongest in the middle — drafting the breakdown, the tasks, and a first pass at dependencies. The two ends — scope and the committed timeline — are where reality lives, and those stay yours.

Run the pipeline once end to end and you have a draft; run it as a conversation — "now break stage 2 further," "re-estimate task 4 assuming half-time staff" — and you have a plan you actually shaped. The value isn't in the first output. It's in steering the stages, and knowing which ones you must not hand off.

AI drew Content (phase 4) running in parallel with Build — good. But it also put Design fully before Build with no overlap. Ask any PM: those phases usually overlap, and forcing them to serialise is the phantom dependency that stretches a timeline. The bars are a draft, not a schedule.

The WBS is the single highest-leverage thing to ask AI for, because a good breakdown makes every later stage easy — you can estimate a small task, but not a vague phase. Get the tree right, prune what you don't need, add what it missed, and the rest of the plan almost falls out of it. Which is exactly why the next stage — estimating — is the one AI gets most wrong.

05 / 06

AI estimates are optimistic — always

Ask AI how long a task takes and it answers as if everything goes right: no sick days, no rework, no waiting on someone else. That's the planning fallacy, baked in. Your job is to translate its clean estimate into a real one — with buffers and a reality check.

AI has read a million project plans and it echoes their best case. Real work has friction: a design goes back twice, a dependency slips, someone is off for a week. The fix isn't to argue with the estimate — it's to apply the same corrections a seasoned PM applies by reflex: a contingency buffer, an integration tax, and a glance at how similar projects actually went. The optimism gap

AI's estimate is a floor, not a forecast. The gap you see is the work it can't imagine — and the work you'll be blamed for missing.

The point isn't that AI is bad at estimating — it's that it estimates the task, while a project runs on everything around the tasks. You don't need AI to be a better optimist. You need to add the friction it structurally cannot see, and commit to the honest number, not the hopeful one.

Spot what AI invented

A plausible plan is more dangerous than an obviously wrong one, because you'll trust it. AI invents tasks that sound reasonable, wires dependencies that don't exist, and silently drops constraints you never restated. Reading the plan for what shouldn't be there is the skill that keeps a project real.

Honest limits — the PM owns the plan, not the AI

AI will draft you a plan in seconds, and that speed is real. But it plans in a frictionless world: timelines with no slack, dependencies that sound logical but aren't, best-practice tasks nobody asked for, and constraints it quietly forgets between messages. None of that is a bug you can prompt away — it's the shape of a tool that has never actually shipped a project. Run this review on every plan before you commit to it.

The point isn't ceremony — it's that a plan which reads well and a plan which is achievable are two different things, and only this review tells them apart. AI removes the tedium of drafting the breakdown. It does not remove your responsibility for the commitment. Draft with AI, commit as a PM.

What to carry forward

You don't build the plan cell by cell anymore — you describe the goal and its constraints, and AI drafts the breakdown. But loading the brief, walking the pipeline, correcting the always-optimistic estimates, and catching what AI invented are skills that stay firmly yours. That's where the value moved.

  • Goal in, plan out · a loaded brief names done, constraints, team, and deadline
  • Walk the pipeline stage by stage · scope → WBS → tasks → estimate → dependencies → timeline
  • The WBS is AI's strongest output — a numbered tree you prune and extend
  • AI's estimates are a floor · add buffers, an integration tax, and a reference-class check
  • Read the plan for what shouldn't be there · invented tasks, phantom deps, dropped constraints
  • AI drafts the plan · the PM owns the commitment
