Week 1 · Lesson 02 · AI Excel

Say what you want. Get the formula.

You no longer need to memorise Excel's function library to use it. Describe the result in plain English, and AI drafts the formula. This lesson teaches the part that still matters: how to describe the problem well, recognise the common patterns, read a formula you inherited, fix its errors — and never trust a formula you haven't tested.

01 / 05

Plain English in, a formula out

The old skill was recalling syntax. The new skill is describing a problem precisely enough that a machine can turn it into syntax. The second is far more learnable — and far more useful.

Here is the shift. You used to type =XLOOKUP(...) from memory, comma by comma, and hope the arguments were in the right order. Now you type a sentence — "look up the price for the item in A2 from the products sheet" — and AI writes the function. But the sentence has to carry enough information: where the data is, what you want back, and what to do when there's no match. 02 / 05

The anatomy of a good formula prompt

A useful formula request answers three questions before AI ever runs: what does the data look like, what do you want out, and what happens at the edges.

Think of it as giving directions. "Take me downtown" gets you a shrug; "take me to 4th and Main, avoid the highway, drop me at the north entrance" gets you there. A formula prompt works the same way. Skip these and the AI guesses — usually reasonably, sometimes disastrously, and always in a way you'll have to double-check anyway.

Part 1

Data shape

Where the data lives and how it's laid out: which columns, whether there are headers, is it one sheet or two, are amounts text or numbers. AI can't see your workbook — describe it.

Part 2

The goal

The exact result you want in the cell, in outcome terms: "the total sales for the East region in Q3," not "do a SUMIFS." Say the what; let AI pick the how.

Part 3

Edge cases

What should happen when there's no match, a blank cell, a divide-by-zero, or a duplicate. State it — "show 0 if not found" — or the AI decides for you.

Part 4

The example

One concrete row beats a paragraph of description. "For A2 = 'Bolt', it should return 4.50 from the Prices sheet" removes almost all ambiguity at once.

Notice that none of these require you to know a single function name. You describe the situation and the outcome; the AI supplies the syntax. That division of labour — you own the intent, AI owns the recall — is the whole method of this lesson, and it repeats in every pattern below.

Five patterns cover almost everything

Spreadsheet work looks endless, but most real formulas fall into a handful of shapes. Learn to recognise the shape, and you'll know what to ask AI for.

You don't need to memorise the syntax on these cards — that's exactly what AI is for. What's worth internalising is the name of the job, so that when a task lands on your desk you can think "ah, this is a lookup" or "this is a conditional sum" and describe it cleanly. "Add up sales where region is East and month is March" is a conditional sum. Name the job, describe the data, mention the edge case — the syntax is the AI's problem, not yours.

05 / 05

Errors are messages, not dead ends

When a cell shows #N/A or #REF!, Excel isn't broken — it's telling you exactly what went wrong, in a code. AI translates the code and, better, suggests the fix.

Beginners see an error and panic or delete the formula. Each error code has a precise meaning and a small set of usual causes. Paste the formula and the error into AI — "this returns #VALUE!, why, and how do I fix it?" — and you'll get the cause plus a corrected version. The discipline that keeps you safe: verify on a test range

AI-drafted formulas are usually right, but "usually" is not "always" — and a wrong formula copied down 10,000 rows is a wrong report sent to your boss. Never accept a formula on faith. Before you trust it across the whole sheet, prove it on a small slice where you already know the answer. Run this six-point check on every formula AI hands you.

The point isn't ceremony — it's that a formula which runs and a formula which is correct are two different things, and only the test range tells them apart. AI removes the tedium of writing syntax. It does not remove your responsibility for the answer. Verify small, then trust big.

What to carry forward

You don't memorise functions anymore — you describe outcomes and let AI supply the syntax. But describing well, recognising the pattern, reading what you inherit, decoding errors, and verifying on a test range are skills that stay firmly yours. That's where the value moved.
