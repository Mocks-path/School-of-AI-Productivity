Week 5 · AI Project Management

AI Tracking, Status & Communication — turn scattered signals into a status people trust

A project isn't behind because of one big surprise — it drifts, quietly, while everyone assumes someone else is watching. This lesson teaches the status workflow AI can accelerate: collect the signals, summarise, flag the real risks, tailor the same facts to an exec and to the team, and turn messy standup notes into owned tasks. Four hands-on drills — plus how to catch the "watermelon" status that looks green and is red inside.

The status workflow — five steps, every update

"Write me a status update" gets you fluent, confident prose that may quietly hide the one thing your manager needed to hear. A trustworthy update follows an order: gather the raw signals first, compress them, surface the risks honestly, shape the message for who's reading, and review before you send. AI can draft any step in seconds — but only you know which blocker is real. Skip the risk step or the review, and you'll send a green light over a red project.

STEP 1
Collect signals
Pull the raw inputs: standup notes, ticket movement, blockers, dates slipping, chat threads. AI can summarise them, but it can only see what you feed it.
→
STEP 2
Summarise
Compress the noise into what changed since last update: done, in progress, next. Facts, not feelings.
→
STEP 3
Flag risks
Name what could slip and why, with a RAG colour and an owner. This is the step AI most wants to soften — don't let it.
→
STEP 4
Tailor to audience
Same facts, two messages: execs want the "so what" and the ask; the team wants specifics and owners.
→
STEP 5
Review & send
Read it as the recipient. Is anything over-reassuring? Did AI mark something "done" that isn't? Verify with the team, then send.
Green — On track
On schedule, no help needed. Reserve it for when it's actually true — over-using green is how projects fail quietly.
Amber — At risk
Something is threatening the date or scope. There's a plan, but it needs attention or a decision soon.
Red — Off track / blocked
A commitment will be missed without intervention. Red isn't failure — it's an honest, early ask for help.

Same facts, right audience — pick the update that fits the reader

A status update isn't one message; it's the same truth shaped for who's reading it. An executive wants the headline, the risk, and what you need from them — in three lines, no jargon. The team wants the specifics: which ticket, which owner, what's next. AI will happily generate one generic update for both — and it lands wrong for everyone. Below are three pairs. In each, pick the version that fits the stated reader.

2

Notes to tasks — every action needs an owner and a date

Meeting notes are where decisions go to die. "We should update the API docs" is a wish; "Priya updates the API docs by Fri" is a task. AI is genuinely good at extracting action items from a transcript — but it invents owners, drops dates, and turns vague talk into fake-crisp tasks. Read the standup notes, then drag each line to where it belongs: a real task (has a clear owner and deadline), needs an owner/date (an action with a gap AI shouldn't guess), or not a task (context, opinion, or something already done). Wrong drops turn red — hover them to learn why.

Read the true colour — Green, Amber or Red?

The most dangerous status is the "watermelon": green on the surface, red on the inside. It happens when an update reassures instead of reports — "great progress," "team is confident," "on track" — while a real blocker hides underneath. For each situation below, pick the honest RAG colour, then check yourself against the PM's verdict. Rule of thumb: if a date or commitment is threatened, it is not green, no matter how the update is worded.

4

Verify before you send — catch AI before it reassures the wrong person

AI drafts a status update in seconds, but it doesn't know your project. It reads "almost done" as "done," it smooths a real blocker into an "on track," and it happily writes a confident summary from notes that were themselves wrong. It never talked to your team. Answer these four — each locks in with the reasoning.

✓

The 60-second review before you hit send

Before any AI-drafted status update leaves your outbox, read it as the person receiving it and run these checks. Most bad updates are caught in a minute — if you actually look.

  • ✓
    Is anything marked "done" that isn't? AI reads "nearly finished" and "in review" as complete. Confirm every "done" with the person who did it before you report it up.
  • !
    Did it flag the real risk — or bury it? If the update is all reassurance and no ask, that's a watermelon. Name the blocker, its owner, and what you need. Red early beats green late.
  • 👤
    Does every action have an owner and a date? "Someone will look into it" is not a task. If AI left a gap, fill it with a real name and day — or ask the team, don't guess.
  • 🎯
    Does it fit the reader? An exec update should open with the "so what" and the ask; a team update needs specifics. One generic message serves neither well.

Quick reflect — make it stick

A sentence each is enough. Writing it in your own words is what makes it stay. (Nothing is saved or sent.)
