Week 4 · Lesson 02 · AI Slides

Describe the talk. Get the deck.

AI will build a slide deck from a sentence — but a sentence produces a generic deck. This lesson teaches the part that turns "make me some slides" into a presentation worth standing behind: how to write a brief that carries audience and goal, how to feed AI a document or an outline, how to give slides a spine instead of a bullet-dump, and how to cut the wordy, invented filler AI loves to add.

01 / 06

A vague ask makes a vague deck

"Make me a presentation about our Q3 results" gets you fifteen forgettable slides. The generator isn't guessing at your talk — it's guessing at everything, because you told it almost nothing.

The shift is the same one from the whole course: you no longer assemble slides by hand, you describe the presentation you want. But a deck brief has to carry more than a topic. It needs the audience you're speaking to, the goal you want them to walk away with, the length, the tone, and ideally the structure and the source material. Drop those in and the AI stops improvising a generic corporate deck. Watch a bare topic and a full brief produce very different decks below.

02 / 06

The six ingredients of a deck brief

A brief that produces a usable first draft answers six questions before AI builds a single slide: who, why, how long, what voice, what shape, and from what.

Think of briefing AI like briefing a designer you've just met. "Make me slides" leaves them to invent your whole context. Hand them these six, and the draft comes back aimed at the right room. You don't need slick prose — a labelled list works fine — but leave one out and the AI quietly decides it for you, usually toward the generic.

Audience

Who's in the room

Executives skimming for the decision, engineers who want the detail, customers who need the benefit. The same content is three different decks depending on who's watching.

Goal

The one takeaway

What should they think, feel, or do when the lights come up? "Approve the budget," "trust the roadmap," "book the demo." State the outcome; the slides serve it.

Length & tone

Time and voice

"Eight slides for a ten-minute standup, plain and direct" versus "twenty for a board review, formal." Length caps rambling; tone stops it sounding like a stranger.

Structure & source

Shape and material

Give it a spine — problem, insight, action — and point it at real material: your notes, a report, a doc. Structure prevents drift; source prevents invention.

Notice that none of these require design skill or knowing your slide tool's menus. You describe the situation and the outcome; the AI drafts the deck. That division — you own the intent and the judgement, AI owns the drafting speed — is the method of this lesson, and it repeats in every figure below.

03 / 06

Prompt, document, or outline?

You can start a deck three ways, and they trade control for effort differently. Knowing which to reach for saves the most time.

AI will build a deck from a one-line prompt, from a document you already wrote, or from an outline you sketch first. Each gives you a different balance of speed, accuracy, and control. The prompt is fastest and loosest; the document is most faithful to real content; the outline gives you the most control over the story. Click through the three to see when each wins.

Figure 2 · Three ways to start a deck

A good rule of thumb: if the content already exists, feed the document — you'll get slides grounded in your real material instead of the AI's imagination. If you're starting cold but you know the story, write the outline first and let AI expand it. Reach for the one-line prompt only for throwaway drafts or when you genuinely want the AI's take on structure.

04 / 06

Give the deck a spine

The difference between a deck that lands and one that's endured is structure. AI defaults to a flat list of topics; you have to ask it for a story.

Left to itself, a generator produces slides that each state a fact and never build toward anything — a bullet-dump. A presentation that persuades has a spine: it sets up a problem, delivers an insight, and points to an action, with one idea carrying each slide. These are the narrative moves worth naming in your brief, so the AI arranges content into an argument instead of a list. Click each card to open it.

Figure 3 · Narrative moves to ask for
Move 01

Problem → Insight → Action

The three-beat spine that turns a report into an argument.

Open on the problem the audience feels, reveal the insight they haven't seen, then name the action you want. Every slide earns its place by advancing one of these three beats.

"Structure this as problem, then insight, then a clear ask — one beat per section."

Move 02

One idea per slide

If a slide needs two headlines, it's two slides.

A slide the audience can grasp in five seconds beats a dense one they read instead of listening to you. Ask AI to split any slide carrying more than a single point.

"Limit each slide to one idea and one headline. Split anything that has two."

Move 03

Story over bullet-dump

A sequence that builds beats a list that just sits there.

Bullets state; stories move. Ask the AI to make each slide set up the next, so the deck reads as one line of reasoning rather than disconnected fragments.

"Make each slide lead into the next so the deck reads as one connected story."

None of these are the AI's default — a generator optimises for covering the topic, not for making a point. So you name the spine in the brief, and you check the draft has one. The structure is the part of the deck that is most yours, because it encodes what you're actually trying to say.

05 / 06

Cut the wordy slide down

AI's biggest slide sin is words. It writes paragraphs where a slide needs a phrase. The fix is one instruction — and the difference is stark.

A raw AI slide often reads like a report page: full sentences, three clauses each, everything the model could think of. But a slide is a visual aid, not a script — the words on screen should support you, not replace you. Ask AI to "cut this to a headline and three short phrases, move the detail to speaker notes," and the slide transforms. Toggle the demo below.

Refine slide by slide, not all at once

The first draft is a starting point, never the final deck. The productive loop is to work one slide at a time — "make slide 3 punchier," "this transition is weak, bridge it," "the ask on the last slide is buried" — accepting and rejecting until each slide earns its place. Where AI reliably lets you down

AI drafts fast, but it has three habits you must correct every time. It pads with generic filler, it over-writes, and — most dangerous — it invents facts that sound plausible. Click each limit below to see what it looks like and the prompt that keeps it honest.

Wordiness

Invented facts

How you fix it

Ask AI

The rule that keeps you safe is simple: AI drafts, you verify — and you never let a number onto a slide you haven't confirmed. A deck that looks polished carries just as much authority when its facts are wrong, which is exactly what makes invented facts the one limit you can't afford to skip. Draft with AI; own the truth of every slide yourself.

What to carry forward

You don't build decks slide by slide anymore — you brief AI and refine what it drafts. But writing the brief, giving the deck a spine, cutting the wordiness, and verifying every claim are skills that stay firmly yours. That's where the value moved.

  • A strong brief carries audience, goal, length, tone, structure, and source
  • Feed a document when content exists · sketch an outline when you know the story
  • Ask for a spine · problem → insight → action, one idea per slide, story over bullets
  • AI over-writes · cut to a headline and phrases, push the detail into speaker notes
  • Edit and verify · AI adds filler, over-writes, and invents facts — you own the truth
